# Geography — Larenne and the Larenne Moor

## The shape of the country

Glenumbra is wet country. The sea-fog comes in off the Iliac Bay in the small
hours and does not always leave, and between the fog and the rain there is a
long season in which the moor is less a place than a condition. Larenne sits in
the middle of it, four days' coach south of nothing much and three days' coach
north of the same, on the road that runs between Daggerfall and Camlorn. Both
cities are real to Larenne chiefly as directions. A man says *he went down to
Daggerfall* the way he might say *he went into the fog*, meaning only that he
is no longer here.

The town is not old by the standards of High Rock, which is to say it is only
about eight hundred years old. It exists because the moor road needed somewhere
to change horses at that particular distance, and because the Anwyl — a brown,
peat-stained, unhurried little water — could be forded there and later bridged.
Everything Larenne is grew out of those two facts. It has a market because the
road brought buyers. It has a chapterhouse because the market brought coin and
coin wants blessing. It has a barony because somebody had to weigh the wool.

## The town

The heart of Larenne is **the Weighing Yard**, which strangers call the market
square and no local ever does. It is cobbled in the middle and mud at the edges,
and at its north end stands the barony's public wool-beam under a slate roof on
four posts — a set of scales as tall as a man, chained, with the Vaudry mark
punched into the brass. Every fleece sold in Larenne is supposed to cross that
beam and pay its fraction. Many do.

The Yard's four sides are, by long habit: the beam and **the Hollow Horn** to
the north; **Vaudry Hall** to the east, a fortified manor house rather than a
castle, with arrow-slits nobody has needed in three hundred years and a good
kitchen garden; **the Larenne Chapterhouse** to the south, whose bell — called
the Matron, though no one remembers why — rings the hours, the high days, and
also, by an arrangement that scandalized a previous prior, the arrival of the
southbound coach; and to the west a row of merchant frontages that changes hands
often enough that nobody bothers naming it.

The Horn itself fronts the Yard with a wide carriage arch through to its coach
yard, and that arch is the most important twenty feet of ground in this story,
because it is where the door is worked. From under it you can see the whole
square, the chapterhouse steps, the road coming in past Peatgate, and who is
waiting for whom.

Out from the Yard run the lanes that do the actual work:

- **Tenter Row**, west and downhill, where the fleeces are washed, fulled and
  stretched on hooked frames. It smells of lanolin and stale piss in all weather
  and everyone pretends it does not. **Coudray's Mill** stands at the bottom of
  it on the Anwyl, and the mill's undershot wheel is the sound Larenne sleeps to.
- **Peatgate**, north, which is not a gate and has not been since the wall came
  down for building stone. There is an arch, a toll-post, and a shed. Everything
  cut from the moor comes in this way and pays a copper the cartload.
- **The Bellows**, east along the road out, where the farriers, wheelwrights and
  the one blacksmith cluster because coaches break and horses throw shoes and
  the trade follows the traffic.
- **Sallow Lane**, behind the Row — cheap lodgings, damp walls, and where most
  of Larenne's handful of non-Bretons sleep. The town's tolerance is genuine,
  ordinary, and it lives on Sallow Lane rather than on the Yard.
- **The Pack Bridge**, three arches, too narrow for two carts, the subject of
  perhaps a quarter of all litigation heard in Vaudry Hall.

Market is Loredas. The great wool fair is in Hearthfire and doubles the town for
a week.

## The moor

**The Larenne Moor** is heather, cotton-grass, bog myrtle, hidden water and
sheep. It is crossed by the **Coach Road** — properly the Bay Road — and by
older ways that predate it and go where they please.

Landmarks, in the order a traveller meets them going south from Peatgate:

**The Black Cuts**, the peat workings, where each household holds a hereditary
turf-plot with a name of its own (Sourbank, the Widow's Room, Gaultier's Long
Cut). Cutting starts after First Seed and the whole town smells of turf smoke
from Frostfall on.

**The Marchstone**, a weathered grey pillar of Direnni work standing crooked in
open ground, its carving worn to suggestion. It marks a boundary nobody now
observes. Sheep scratch against it. Children are told not to sleep in its shadow,
and are not told why, because the reason has been lost and only the telling
survives.

**The Sisters**, a stand of old wind-crippled thorn and oak in a fold of the
ground, the only real trees for miles. The chapterhouse maintains there are nine
of them, this being a decent number. Nobody who has counted has ever got nine.

**The Widow's Stone**, at the sixth milestone: a niched wayside marker where
coachmen leave a copper against foul weather. The chapterhouse calls it a shrine
of Kynareth. The drivers call it the Widow's Stone. Both are correct in their
fashion and neither party presses the point.

**The Drown**, off the road to the west — a soft place in the moss that takes
sheep and, twice in living memory, men. It is not in the same place it was in
the last generation's stories, which is the honest and unsettling part.

**The Hare's Road** is not on any map. It leaves the Anwyl below Coudray's Mill,
goes round the back of the moor by dry ridges, and rejoins the Bay Road well
south of the Peatgate toll-post. Wool that has not crossed the wool-beam travels
it, and so does brandy that has not paid its duty. Everyone knows. It is, in
Larenne's view, less a crime than a form of weather.

South, a long day's drive, is **Malmont Cross**, the next change-house, where
the moor gives out into hedge and orchard country and the road bears away for
Daggerfall. North, over a toll-bridge, is **Stanchgate**, and beyond that the
road to Camlorn.

## The Dower House

Two miles off the Bay Road, up an avenue of limes, stands the Vaudry family's
own **Dower House**. It is not a stranger's manor — it is the Baroness's own,
shut up eighteen years ago the season her elder brother Jocelin Vaudry
renounced his claim and went down to Daggerfall to take orders. Letters came
for a year and then stopped. The house has stood shuttered ever since — the
chapterhouse's copy of the deed is incomplete, a transfer with a signature and
no named grantee, one of the documents Brother Aumery would very much like an
afternoon with.

The facts, such as Larenne has them, are these. No smoke from the chimneys. No
lights. No servants seen in the town, no orders placed with any Larenne merchant,
no tithe paid and none demanded. And yet the avenue is kept clear, the gates are
kept sound, the deer park wall is mended where it falls, and every year at
Evening Star a factor comes up from Daggerfall and pays the barony's land-dues
to the penny, in coin that is always a little older than the coin in general
circulation. He signs, he takes his receipt, he goes.

The shepherds put their flocks elsewhere. Not from fear, they will tell you —
the grazing is simply poor up there, and the dogs won't work well, and it is a
long way back in the dark. The moor road runs past the gate-lodge and everybody
who walks it walks a little faster on that stretch, and would be embarrassed to
be asked why.
