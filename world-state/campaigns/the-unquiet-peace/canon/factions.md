# Factions — Who Holds What In Larenne

Larenne has perhaps four hundred hearths, a market charter, and one road worth
mentioning. Nobody in it is powerful. What people have instead is standing — a
position in a web of debts, favours, tithes, and ninety-year grudges that everyone
can recite and nobody wrote down. A doorsman at the Hollow Horn sees more of that
web in a week than the Baroness sees in a year, because all of it eventually walks
past him to get a drink.

Two things should be true of every faction scene in this campaign. First: the
stakes are real to the people in them and small to anyone else, and the narration
should take the people's side, not the outsider's. Second: the PC is inside this
web without being a member of it. He is the Argonian at the Horn. Everyone knows
where he stands at the door; nobody quite knows where he stands.

## The Hollow Horn

The Horn is not a tavern. It is a licensed change-house on the Daggerfall coaching
road, which means it holds the **post-right**: the coach stops here, the teams are
swapped here, the mail bag is signed for here, and every passenger who wants a bed
between Daggerfall and the Wrothgarian road takes one under this roof or sleeps in
the coach. The taproom is the smaller half of the business. The stableyard is the
larger. The post-right is the whole of it — without the right, the Horn is a
moorland alehouse with eleven beds it cannot fill.

**Mistress Perrine Aubrec** has held the Horn for nineteen years, since her husband
Gerauld died of a chest complaint in a wet spring. She is somewhere past fifty, very
short, and has the flat unhurried competence of a woman who has never once in her
life been surprised by an emergency. She keeps the house ledger in a private
shorthand of her own devising — hooks and bars, no words — and she is entirely
aware that this makes her the only person alive who knows what the Horn is worth.
She hired the PC herself, off the yard, over the objections of two aldermen and her
own niece, and has never explained why beyond *he was standing still and everyone
else was shouting*. She does not praise him. She has also never once docked his
wage for a broken bench, and both of them understand that this is the same thing.

**Toussaint Merigot**, called Touss, is head ostler and has been in that yard forty
years under three owners. He is deaf in the left ear and answers questions he has
half-heard with whatever he wanted to say anyway. He can name a coach team by the
sound of its gait through the arch before it is in the yard, and does, aloud, to
nobody. Touss is the one man in Larenne who has never in the PC's hearing referred
to him as *the Argonian* — not out of principle, but because he sorts people the
way he sorts horses, by whether they are any use, and has long since filed the PC
under *use*.

**Nolwenn Aubrec** is Perrine's niece, thirty-one, and runs the kitchen, the linen,
and as much of the accounting as her aunt permits — which is the taproom slate and
nothing else. Nolwenn expects to inherit the Horn. She is not certain she will.
Perrine has never said so, has a living brother in Wayrest with sons, and enjoys not
saying so more than is entirely kind. This is the quiet ache underneath every one of
Nolwenn's small tyrannies about the pantry, and it makes her sharp with anyone her
aunt visibly favours, the PC included. She is not cruel. She is simply nineteen years
into a job that might turn out to have been somebody else's all along.

**Ambrose Cathelin**, the pot-boy, is fourteen, called Broz, and hero-worships the
PC with the total absence of tact peculiar to that age. He asks about Black Marsh
constantly — about the trees, about eggs, about whether it is true they eat mud —
and the PC does not have the answers, which Broz has never noticed, because the PC
has become very good at giving him a story instead of a fact.

The Horn's regulars are its other half. **Dame Marguerite Osse**, the Widow Osse,
takes the corner settle every market day and has done for twenty-two years; she is
the town's chief wool-factor and the actual price of fleece in Larenne is whatever
she says it is over a cup of hot ale between the eighth and ninth hour. **Hanryk
Sowe** is a Nord, drives the Daggerfall run, arrives every ninth day with the news,
the mail, and a running tab that Perrine allows to grow to exactly forty drakes and
never one more. **Ysore Cabray** sells charms out of the snug — a hedge-wife of the
usual Glenumbra sort, Direnni-descended if you believe her mother, and she keeps the
Horn's cold-larder charm renewed twice a year for the price of her seat.

The Horn's live trouble is this: **Guiot Malebranche**, who keeps the Blue Mare at
Trevec Cross on the far side of the moor, has petitioned the barony to move the
post-right to his house, on the grounds that the moor road floods below Larenne four
weeks in the year and the coach has twice been turned back. It is not a bad
argument. That is what frightens Perrine about it.

## The Barony of Larenne

The barony is sworn to the Crown of Daggerfall and hears from it about as often as
the weather changes — which is to say constantly and without much consequence, until
suddenly with a great deal of consequence.

**Baroness Isabeau Vaudry** holds Larenne in her own right, her husband Ottevin
having been dead eleven years. She is fifty-four, wears mourning grey out of habit
rather than grief, and has the disconcerting Vaudry manner of asking a question as
though she expects a true answer — including of stablehands, including of Argonians,
which unnerves people far more than contempt would. She is neither kind nor unkind.
She is a woman with a ledger problem.

The ledger problem is the **hearth-roll**. Every twelfth year the Daggerfall
exchequer counts the barony's hearths and sets its dues by the count. Larenne's last
roll was taken in the year after the flood, when three lanes stood empty and half the
carters were wintering elsewhere, and the town has been paying on that undercount ever
since — legally, blamelessly, and with enormous relief. The new roll is due. An honest
count would raise Larenne's dues by something near a third, and the Baroness has
exactly one place to find a third: the market tolls. Which is to say from the
wool-factors, the stallholders, and the post-right at the Hollow Horn.

So the whole town is now engaged in a shared, low-grade, entirely mundane conspiracy
about arithmetic. Is a stable loft a hearth if a man sleeps in it? Are the Horn's
eleven letting-rooms hearths, or one hearth? Is the almshouse a hearth or a charity?
Nobody will lie outright. Everybody will be extremely helpful about definitions.
**Serjeant-Assessor Guiscard Thenn** has been sent from Daggerfall to settle it, and
he is lodging — because there is nowhere else — at the Hollow Horn, where the
doorsman takes his coat every evening and where he has already begun asking mild,
patient, extremely well-aimed questions of the staff.

The succession is the barony's other unfinished sentence. **Amaury Vaudry**, the son,
is twenty-three, likeable, an adequate rider, and wants very badly to be somewhere
that is not Larenne — Daggerfall, a knightly order, anywhere with a name. **Sibylle
Vaudry**, nineteen, has read the moor-law, understands the turbary rolls better than
the reeve does, and is the one the tenants go to when they want a thing actually
fixed. Breton custom favours Amaury. Everyone in the household knows what everyone in
the household knows, and nobody says it, and it sits at the Vaudry table every
evening like a fourth chair.

Outward, the barony has two faces. **Reeve Osmond Cabray** collects the tolls, keeps
the market peace, and is cousin to the hedge-wife in the Horn's snug; he is tired,
broadly fair, and can be relied upon to let the Horn's benches spill onto the square
on fair days in exchange for a keg finding its way to his kitchen, an arrangement so
old it has stopped being corruption and become custom. **Serjeant Aloys Bec**
commands the town watch, which is four men, two of them his cousins, and a bell. Bec's
private policy — never spoken, never written, never acknowledged — is that trouble at
the Hollow Horn is handled by the Hollow Horn's doorsman, and that this is best for
everyone. He has bought the PC a drink for this exactly once, and did not say what it
was for.

Beyond the town wall, **Ser Ranulf Coudrec of Wethermarch** holds a knight's fee on
the moor's western edge and has been arguing with the Vaudrys about peat since before
either family's current generation was born. The dispute is the turbary rights on
Cullen's Wet: whose tenants may cut, how many spades' breadth, and in which weeks.
Ninety years of it. It has produced perhaps six bloodied noses in the Horn's yard on
market days, one broken cart, and a truly extraordinary quantity of documentation.
Coudrec has lately begun hinting that he might take it over the Baroness's head to a
Daggerfall court, which is the single most aggressive thing anyone in this parish has
done in a decade.

## The Larenne Chapterhouse

A minor house of the Eight — the Nine, officially, since the new altar went up, and
the older half of Larenne still says Eight and will go on saying it until they die.
The chapterhouse's real business is not devotion. It is the almshouse, the infirmary,
the burial ground, and the records: births, deaths, tithes, wills, and the parish's
memory of itself.

**Prior Faustin Belloc** runs it, is past seventy, and has narrowed his personal
religion down over the years to Stendarr and the almshouse loaves, which he considers
the same subject. He is the Baroness's chaplain by courtesy and her creditor by
accident — the chapterhouse has been carrying the barony's tithe arrears for two years
without mentioning it, and the Prior's great ambition is to convert that unmentioned
debt into a Julianos scriptorium before he dies. This puts him directly in the path
of the hearth-roll, and he knows it.

**Sister Ysolt Meaune** keeps the infirmary and is the nearest thing Larenne has to a
physician: restoration for what restoration answers, and vinegar, splints and honest
opinion for everything else. She has patched the PC's knuckles perhaps a dozen times.
She has also written in her day-book, in the ordinary way she notes anything, that the
Argonian at the Horn does not startle — not that he is brave, that he does not startle,
which is a different observation — and she has not thought about it since.

**Brother Aumery** is the chapterhouse's chronicler and its least dignified asset: a
scribe of no rank and boundless curiosity who illustrates the town's notable happenings
in the margins of ledgers that are supposed to contain grain accounts, and who cannot
stop himself adding captions. The Prior has told him to stop. The Prior has also
quietly kept every page.
