# The History of Larenne

## What the Name Means, Depending on Whom You Ask

Larenne has two histories, and they do not like each other.

The chapterhouse keeps the first one in a bound assize roll with a cracked spine, and it says that the name is *Lar-Renne*, which is elvish, or was, and means the Lady's Rest — that in the days when the Direnni held High Rock from their island in the bay, some highborn Aldmeri woman broke her journey here and found the place restful. Brother Aumery will show you the line if you let him. He will also, if pressed, admit that the hand that wrote it wrote it four hundred years after the fact, and that the man who wrote it was being paid by a baron who wanted an elvish pedigree for a town that had none.

The drovers keep the second history, which is not written anywhere, and it says that a *laren* is simply the old moor-word for the firm place — the gravel shelf where the cart stops sinking. There are peat bogs to the north and peat bogs to the south and between them a mile and a half of hard ground, and that is the whole reason there is a town here. Not a battle. Not a saint. Just the only dry place to stop.

Both stories are told at the Hollow Horn most nights, usually by the same people, and the version a man tells depends on how much he has drunk and whom he is trying to impress.

## The Old Wars, Which Happened Somewhere Else

Larenne claims a battle. Everyone in Glenumbra claims that battle. Somewhere out on these moors, in the First Era, the Direnni broke an Alessian host — the story is real enough, the ground is disputed, and every parish between here and Camlorn has a hummock it swears is the grave-mound. Larenne's is out past Ninebank, and the peat-cutters turn up iron from it, and the iron is almost always a horseshoe. Once in a generation it is something better, and then it goes to the chapterhouse and is called elf-iron by people who have never seen elf-iron.

What the town actually remembers, in its bones, is not the First Era. It is the long Second Era gap when there was no Empire and no law on the road, and the drove-trade went armed or did not go at all. That is when the horn was hung.

## The Horn

The Hollow Horn is named for the thing over its door: a great ox-horn, hollowed, banded in tarnished silver, cracked from lip to tip so that it will not sound. In the bad centuries a watchman kept it on the roof-ridge and blew it when the road came up clear — one long note meaning the drove could move, no note at all meaning stay behind the wall and say nothing. It has not been sounded in three hundred years, because it cannot be, and because nobody has wanted to. There is a joke about it in Larenne, worn smooth with use, which is that if you ever do hear the Horn, you should not stop to ask who blew it. People laugh. They laugh a little less at three in the morning.

The same horn is also, legally, the town's ale measure. The Kingdom of Daggerfall's market charter — early in the Peace, when the Septim line was mending roads and selling charters to pay for it — set Larenne's lawful pint by the capacity of a horn-measure kept at the sign of the Horn, and the innkeeper is technically an officer of the market on that account, and gets a shilling a year for it, and has been getting the same shilling since the coin was worth something.

## How the Kingdom Touches Larenne (Barely)

Larenne is a barony sworn to Daggerfall. In practice this means three things, and only three.

It means the levy, which was commuted to coin four generations back — so many pounds a year in place of so many men-at-arms, a bargain that seemed shrewd when it was struck and now merely seems old. It means the assize, a royal justiciar who rides the circuit every third autumn, sits two days in the chapterhouse hall, hears whatever the barony has not managed to settle, and rules on fences, wells, bastardy and peat. And it means the wool contract, which is not the Kingdom's business at all but which everyone treats as though it were, because the wool goes to Daggerfall and the price comes back from Daggerfall and there is no arguing with either.

There is also, technically, a boundary dispute. The moor-edge north of Ninebank has been *under review* between Larenne and its neighbours for something over eighty years, which means the bridge-toll there has not been lawfully collected in living memory, which means it is collected anyway by whoever gets to the bridge first. Nobody wants it resolved. A resolved dispute would have an owner, and an owner would have to pay for the bridge.

Beyond that: the Empire is a thing that happens to other places. A legion detachment came through once, on its way somewhere it did not explain, and bought every barrel in town and paid fairly, and that is the most the Emperor has ever done for Larenne. The Peace is a word people use with a flat little irony, the way you speak of a landlord who has not yet come to look at the roof.

## The Vaudrys, and the Thing Everyone Knows About Them

The Vaudrys were not always the barony. Three generations back they were wool-factors — buyers, weighers, men with scales — and they married into the title, and the two or three old families in Larenne have never entirely stopped calling them the wool-lords, though only among themselves and only after the second cup.

Then there was the annulment. Baron Alric Vaudry married Ysabeau Corvenne of Daggerfall for her father's money, spent a good portion of it, and had the marriage undone some three years later on a point of consanguinity certified — after long and prayerful consideration — by the Larenne chapterhouse, which acquired a new lead roof that same summer. The roof is still on. So is Ysabeau Corvenne. She never went home. She lives in the tall house on Market Row, comes to the Hollow Horn on the first of each month, takes one glass of Wayrest wine, and sits at the window from which one can see the barony's gate. She has done this for something near forty years. The Corvennes still hold the wool contract, and Larenne's wool still fetches a little less than Camlorn's, and nobody in town believes for one moment that those two facts are unrelated.

The present Baroness Vaudry came to the seat sideways. She was the second child. Her elder brother Jocelin renounced his claim and went to Daggerfall to take orders, or to take something, and was not seen again; letters came for a year and then stopped. The Vaudry dower-house out on the moor road — the Dower House, everyone calls it, as though there were only one — was shut up that same season and has been shut ever since. The shutters get repainted. Nobody is quite sure who pays for that.

## The Years People Count By

Ask anyone in Larenne how long ago a thing happened and they will not give you a year. They will give you one of these.

**The Wet Years.** Three seasons of rain, close to forty years gone. The oats drowned in the ground, the sheep took the water-rot, and the barony opened its granary — and charged for it, at a price that was not quite robbery and was very much not charity. Grandmothers still say *at granary prices* to mean a favour that came with a bill. It is the single deepest grudge in the town and it is nobody's fault living, which makes it worse.

**The Malt Fire.** Some twenty-five years back, a fire started in the brewhouse behind Market Row on a dry night with a north wind and took a third of the row before the bucket-line stopped it. Two dead, both of them in the row and neither of them named in the telling any more. The barony's ordinance afterward required brick on the market side, which is why one flank of the square is brick and hard-edged and the other is old leaning timber, and why the Hollow Horn's north wing does not match the rest of the Hollow Horn.

And the blame fell on a Khajiit sugar-tinker who had pitched by the brewhouse and moved on two days before it happened. He was never found and never charged and it has never mattered; the caravans understood the shape of it and stopped coming, and Larenne has been a town that outsiders pass through rather than trade in ever since. The town does not think of itself as having done anything. That is precisely the point. There are perhaps four people alive who could tell you about a brewer's apprentice, a lamp, and a spilled tun, and none of them ever has.

**The Ninebank Judgment.** Twenty-odd years ago the assize ruled on the turbary — the peat-cutting rights — at Ninebank, between the Perrenots and the Gascels, and the ruling exists, in ink, in oilcloth, and is produced annually. It has never once been obeyed. Both families cut the same bank on the first day of cutting, every year, and there is a fight, and the fight has become so exact in its steps — the same shouting, the same shoving, the same one broken tool — that the town turns out to watch it the way it turns out for a feast day. The Kingdom considers the matter settled. Larenne considers it a tradition.

**The Ninebank Man.** Nine years ago the cutters brought up a body out of the deep peat: a man, tanned brown as saddle-leather, hair still on him, a twisted cord still around his throat. Older than the Empire, the chapterhouse said, and probably older than that. Brother Aumery drew him and buried him in the chapterhouse yard with the Eight named over him and the ninth niche's god named too, quietly, at the end, because one does not know what such a man would have wanted. What the bog teaches Larenne, and what Larenne would rather not have been taught, is that this ground does not let things rot. It keeps them. It keeps them whole, and it gives them back on its own schedule, and the giving back is never asked for.

## The Argonian at the Horn

There has been one other Argonian in Larenne's living memory: a bargeman who worked a season on the coast road and went south again before the frost, and whose name three different people will give you three different versions of. Then there is the doorsman at the Hollow Horn, who has been there long enough that the market children do not stare, long enough to be owed money by half the regulars, long enough to be *the Argonian at the Horn* — which is a place in the town, and a warm one, and is not the same thing as being from here.

Larenne's history with outsiders is not cruel. It is worse than cruel; it is *conditional*. The town remembers the Malt Fire without ever saying what it remembers about it, and the memory sits under the floor of every kindness the town extends, the way the peat sits under the gravel shelf, holding whatever it was given, waiting to be cut.
