# Magic in Larenne — The Unquiet Peace

## 1. Hedge-work: magic as a trade, not a wonder

In Glenumbra, magic is a service one purchases, like thatching or shoeing. Bretons
are the descendants of Direnni elves and Nedic farmers, and their part of that
inheritance shows up less as glory than as competence: half the households on the
Larenne moor own at least one charm they did not make themselves, and know
roughly what it cost.

The town's practitioner is Ysolt Fauvel, called the charm-wife, who keeps the
Rushlight House at the moor edge past Cordwain Row. Her work is a ledger of small
necessities. Damp-wards cut into the door-lintels of the wool sheds, because the
moor fog gets into fleece and rots a season's profit in a fortnight. A setting-charm
for a broken arm, which does not knit the bone but stops the swelling long enough
for the bone to be set properly by hand. Sheep-finding, at a rate per head. A rune
scratched under the Hollow Horn's ale-cellar shelf that keeps the cool in and which
the innkeeper renews every second Sun's Dusk, grumbling about the price. This is
Restoration and Alteration, the two respectable schools, taught the hedge way —
by imitation, by drill, by a decade of standing in someone's kitchen doing it wrong.

Two things are said of Breton magic that are worth saying plainly. The first is that
Bretons shrug off hedge-curses the way other folk shrug off weather; a bad charm
thrown at a Larenne carter will usually simply fail to bite, and everyone knows it,
which is why Larenne is a town where people threaten each other with the law
rather than with witchcraft. This protection is a Breton inheritance, in the blood,
and it does not extend to the Argonian on the Hollow Horn's door. He has no such
armor. Nobody has told him so, because to a Breton it is not a fact worth
mentioning, the way one does not mention that water is wet.

The second is that the Larenne Chapterhouse offers Restoration on high days and
for the poor, dispensed by the resident divine of Stendarr with the same brisk
charity as bread. Temple healing here is unremarkable and slightly rationed.
Brother Aumery, who keeps the chapterhouse ledgers and cannot stop drawing in
their margins, records every dispensation, and considers this the least interesting
part of his work.

Hedge-teaching has its own vocabulary, and the GM should use it rather than
game-words. A practitioner's capacity is *the draw* — how much a body can pull
before the hands shake and the vision silvers at the edges. A spell as taught is
*a figure*, meaning both its shape and its sequence of steps. To practice a figure
without spending any draw is *reciting cold*, which is what an honest teacher makes
an apprentice do for months before letting them spend a single measure. And every
figure has two halves, the making and *the closing* — and the closing is the half
that amateurs skip.

## 2. The Veiling Arts, and why nobody in Larenne will teach them

Illusion, in the hedge tradition, is called the Veiling Arts, and it has a bad name in
High Rock for the obvious reason: it is the school of the man who was not there.
Alteration moves the world; Restoration mends it; Illusion lies to the people in it.
A charm-wife who takes Illusion coin loses her Restoration custom, because nobody
will let a known veiler set their daughter's arm. Ysolt Fauvel knows the first two
figures and has not spoken them aloud in twenty years.

The tradition names its progression as five figures, and every practitioner from
Wayrest to Sentinel knows the ladder even if they can only climb the first rung:

- **The Candle** — a false appearance, one sense, one observer, held briefly. The
  standard drill is to make a lit candle look unlit to a single watcher across a table.
  Harmless, tedious, and the foundation of everything above it.
- **The Muffle** — the quieting of sound around a body. Coach-guards use it to move
  a spooked team past a bandit camp. Burglars use it for the other obvious thing.
- **The Veil** — true invisibility, brief and fragile, broken by a hard footfall, a
  shout, or a hand laid on another person. Expensive in draw. Universally
  understood to be the tool of thieves, spies, and worse.
- **The Sway** — the family of figures that work on the mind rather than the eye:
  calm, fear, the small persuasive glamour that makes a stranger's face read as
  familiar. Road-wardens use Calm on horses and swear that is all they use it on.
- **The Still Hand** — paralysis. The stopping of a body in place while its owner
  remains inside it, awake. There is no honest trade that requires the Still Hand.
  It is not taught in Glenumbra. It is taught, when it is taught, by people who want
  something in exchange, and the exchange is never money.

**The sticking.** The reason Illusion frightens people here is not the lying. It is
what happens when an untaught practitioner cannot perform the closing. A figure
that will not close does not simply fail; it settles onto the one who made it. This is
called *a sticking*, and it is the folk-horror engine of magic in this campaign. A
stuck Muffle leaves the caster deaf — first for hours, then for days, and if repeated,
for good. A stuck Veil leaves parts of a body out of step with the light: a hand that
arrives a half-beat after the arm, a reflection that turns its head a moment late. A
stuck Sway leaves the caster carrying the emotion themselves — three days of a
dread with no object, or a placid calm while the barn burns. A stuck Still Hand
locks a jaw, a finger, a knee, and it does not always come back. Stickings are not
usually lethal. They are permanent, visible, and they make a person into a story the
town tells. That is worse, in Larenne, than a wound.

## 3. Where an ordinary doorsman would actually start

There is no Mages Guild in Larenne. The nearest halls are in Daggerfall and
Wayrest, both of which require a sponsor, a fee, and a letter, and none of which
have ever admitted an Argonian doorsman from a coaching inn. So the path in
Larenne runs through three doors, all of them narrow, and one of them badly hung.

**The bad book.** Perrin Guisse keeps a copying-room off the chapterhouse yard,
where he transcribes contracts, wills, and — for a few coppers — chapbooks for
people who cannot afford real ones. Among his stock is *The Wardrobe of Mist,
being a Discourse upon Illusion for Persons of Modest Ability*, attributed to
Magister Ilaine Corbeau of Wayrest. Perrin's exemplar was copied from a copy. It is
missing the sixth chapter. The sixth chapter is the one on the closing. A determined
reader can absolutely get the Candle out of it, and probably the Muffle, and this is
precisely the danger: the book works well enough to be believed. Perrin himself is
deaf in the left ear and hears his own voice as though through a wall, and has been
since he was nineteen, and he will tell anyone who asks that this is what the moor
damp does to a man. It is not.

**The charm-wife.** Ysolt Fauvel will not teach the Veiling Arts. She will teach the
part underneath them — the draw, reciting cold, and above all the closing — because
she would rather train a fool than bury one, and because a doorsman who reads
faces all night is useful to her. Her price is not coin. She wants to know who comes
through the Hollow Horn's door and when: names, coaches, which travelers ask
directions to the moor road. She has her reasons and will not give them. Paying her
means quietly trading on the inn's trust, which is the PC's only real standing in this
town, and that is exactly the point of the price.

**The road-trade.** Four or five times a season a Hammerfell coachwarden named
Hazruun of Tigonus overnights in the Hollow Horn's yard on the Daggerfall–Wayrest
run. She is a Redguard who practices magic, which among her own people is a small
disgrace she has stopped explaining. She learned the Veiling Arts as tools, from
guards, for the road: the Muffle to move a team past trouble, the Sway to settle
horses, the Veil to sit still in a hedge for six hours while men with knives walk past.
She teaches nothing to anyone who has not ridden with her, because she does not
believe a figure is learned until it has been used with something at stake. Riding
with her means leaving Larenne, leaving the door, and taking a road on which
things genuinely happen. She is also, though she will not say so, riding that
particular stretch on purpose: a coach she warded went out onto the moor road
three winters ago and was never found, not the horses, not the wheels, not the
mud-marks, and she has been coming back ever since.

**The locked shelf.** Above Larenne's Wool Hall, the Direnni Cousinry of Glenumbra
meets four times a year — a subscription society of minor gentry who pay dues to be
told they have elven ancestors. Most of it is claret and genealogy. But the Cousinry
owns a locked press of copied texts, and on the second shelf are three volumes of
genuine Direnni-descended Illusion practice, including a complete Corbeau with the
sixth chapter intact. Aumeric Delance, who presides and who claims cousinhood
with both the Vaudrys and the Direnni on evidence he prefers not to produce,
decides who reads it. He will not decide in favor of an Argonian. He might be
persuaded, bought, blackmailed, or simply burgled — and the Wool Hall's upper
room is exactly the sort of place a man who has just learned the Muffle would be
tempted to test it, which is how apprentices get stickings and criminal records in
the same night.

None of these doors gives anything away. Each costs standing, secrecy, service, or
skin, and the GM should make the PC pay before any figure is added to his name.

## 4. The green murmur

There is a thing the PC has that has no name in Glenumbra and that he has never
tried to name himself.

It is not a spell and it cannot be cast. It has no figure, no closing, no draw. It does
not answer when he wants it and it does not announce itself when it comes. What it
does is this: sometimes a room goes very quiet inside him just before it goes loud
outside him. Sometimes his hand is already where it needs to be. Sometimes he
dreams of trees — enormous, pale-barked, breathing trees standing in warm black
water — and the dream has a smell, and the smell is not one he can place, and he
does not mention it to anyone, and he has never mentioned it to anyone, and he
could not say why not.

Argonians call this private undercurrent *the green murmur*, and those few who
speak of it at all say it is the Hist, and say nothing further. There are no Argonians
in Larenne. There is no one within two hundred miles who could tell him whether
what he has is ordinary.

He also has a mark on his hide that he has always had and has always had an
explanation for — an explanation given to him young, by someone who meant well,
that he has never had cause to doubt.

**GM discipline.** The green murmur is not a mechanic and must never become one.
The player cannot invoke it, roll for it, or spend anything on it. It surfaces at the
GM's deliberate choosing, rarely, and always deniably — a stillness someone else
notices and remarks on, a reflex that lands a half-second before thought, a dream
noted in a single unhurried sentence and then dropped. Do not explain it in
narration. Do not have an NPC name it. Do not connect it to any organization, to
the mark, or to the training arc above. If it seems to make the Veiling Arts come
strangely easily to him, that is a thing the reader may notice; it is not a thing the
text says.

## 5. Clan Montalion, at a distance

Vampirism in the Iliac Bay is not a disease so much as a lineage. Eight bloodlines
share this coast, each with its own habits, its own inherited gifts, and its own long
grudges with the other seven, and all eight prefer a quiet arrangement to open
predation — a stable territory, a compliant village, a century of nobody counting
carefully. Clan Montalion holds the Glenumbra countryside by that kind of
arrangement, which is to say that Larenne has never seen one and has stories
anyway: a family on the moor road who went away one autumn and left the shutters
closed, a great-uncle in someone's grandmother's telling who did not age between
two weddings, the standing local advice that nobody sensible walks the moor road
after full dark, given without any reason attached because the reason was forgotten
generations ago and the advice was kept. The old manor out past the dike is
supposedly empty. The chapterhouse does not keep it on the tithe rolls, which is odd,
and Brother Aumery has noticed this, and has done nothing about it.

Two practical notes. Montalion have no appetite for Argonian blood — cold, wrong,
by their reckoning barely worth the trouble — which means the PC could walk that
road for years and be ignored, and which is precisely why the thread can sit
untouched indefinitely. And this stays rumor unless the player steers into it: a name
overheard, a coach that never arrived, an unaccountable gap in a ledger. Never a
confrontation, never an offer, until the player reaches for it.
