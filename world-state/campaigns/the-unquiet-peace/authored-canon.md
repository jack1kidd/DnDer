# The Unquiet Peace

Tone: Quiet dread and slow-dawning destiny, weighted deliberately toward the uncanny — even ordinary inn scenes should carry a faint undercurrent of wrongness (a too-long stillness, a dream half-remembered, the moor at the edge of town) rather than staying purely cozy. Political intrigue (Barony, Chapterhouse) is real but secondary to dread; warmth and levity exist but are the minority note, not the baseline. Numeric approximation for campaign-overview.json: horror/dread ~35, drama/intrigue ~55, comedy/warmth ~10.
Themes: An inherited self, not yet recognized as inherited, The ordinary violence and warmth of service work — seeing a town from its threshold, Belonging as performance: being 'the Argonian at the Horn' rather than being known, Peace as policy, not resolution — and what quietly persists underneath a peace, The ground kills more people here than any monster does, A town that holds a superstition and a denial of it at the same time, in the same mouth, Courtesy as the polished face of exclusion — a town too well-mannered to be cruel and too well-mannered to let him in, A culture you inherit but were never taught, surfacing as habit rather than knowledge, Kinship questions asked in the wrong shape, and the loneliness of having no way to correct them, The daily hours of service work as the real anthropology: bells, chalk marks, wash-water, and who is expected to explain himself, Small government at close range: rule as arithmetic, custom and favour rather than force, Inheritance as an unfinished sentence — the Horn's kitchen and the Baroness's table asking the same question, A place explained by its road and its water before it is explained by its people, Ordinary land carrying old marks nobody can read any more, What a town chooses not to look at, and the practical reasons it gives instead, A town that counts its years in grievances rather than reigns, Peace as the absence of events, and the strange pressure that absence puts on small ones, What the bog keeps: nothing here rots, it only waits to be cut up, Belonging as a verdict the town can revisit without ever announcing it, Skill as a debt: nothing worth knowing here is given, and every teacher's price is paid in standing rather than coin, The cost of self-teaching — the shortcut that works well enough to be believed, Respectable magic and disreputable magic, divided by nothing but what people assume you want it for, A power he carries and cannot use, running quietly alongside a power he is choosing to earn
Voice: Le Guin's measured, anthropological cadence: sentences that take their time, custom explained through its cost rather than named outright, dialogue spare enough that it carries weight. Violence, when it comes, is stated plainly and briefly — its aftermath is where the narration lingers. The PC's own uncanny moments (a stillness that unsettles a room, a reflex that surprises him) are narrated the same patient way as everything else — never flagged with horror-movie emphasis, just quietly noted, the way the narrator notices everything else.

# Bestiary — What Lives On The Larenne Moor

Glenumbra is not a wilderness. It is grazed, cut for peat, walked by drovers, and
crossed twice a week by the Daggerfall coach. Nothing on the Larenne moor is
waiting to be slain by heroes. Most of what can kill a man here is either an
animal defending itself, weather, or the ground.

Keep this small. A doorsman at the Hollow Horn will hear about these creatures far
more often than he will meet one — they belong in the taproom, in complaints about
lost ewes and a carter's broken leg, in what mothers tell children about the causeway
after dark. When one does step into a scene, it should be sudden, brief, and
disproportionately serious, then over. This is not a rotation of set-piece monsters.

## Beasts, plainly

**The hedge pack.** Moor wolves, lean and grey-brown as the gorse, denning somewhere
north of the peat-cuts. In summer they take lambs and are cursed at. In a hard
winter they come down to the hedgerows along the coaching road and follow lone
travellers for miles without ever quite closing — which is worse than being attacked,
and which most Larenne folk will tell you they have experienced personally, whether or
not they have. A lone walker at dusk in Sun's Dusk or Evening Star has a real chance
of dying out there. A party with a fire and a spear does not.

**Gorse-boar.** The animal that actually kills Larenne men. Old boars go blind-mean in
the whin thickets south of town; they weigh what a man weighs and travel faster,
and a tusk opens a thigh in a way the chapterhouse's restoration can only half mend.
The barony licenses two boar-hunts a year, both of which end at the Horn, both of
which have at some point ended with someone carried in.

**Mire adder.** Short, dun, patterned like wet bracken, and lethargic enough that
almost every bite in the parish has been the bite of someone who stepped on it during
hay-cutting. Seldom kills a grown adult; reliably ruins a fortnight, and reliably ruins
a child. Peat-cutters carry a length of cord to bind an arm above the bite and
consider this sufficient medicine.

**Gallow-rooks.** The moor's carrion crows, glossy and unhurried, notable only because
Larenne reads them: rooks circling a particular stretch of the moor road means someone
should go and look, and by long custom somebody does. They are the town's alarm bell
and its gossip in equal measure.

## Ground and weather, which count as beasts here

**The peat-sinks and the haar.** Cut peat leaves black water under a skin of moss, and
the sea-fog comes off the Iliac in an hour and takes the world down to six feet. The
causeway stones are laid for exactly this reason and stepping off them is how most
people who die on the moor die. No violence, no monster — just cold water, a wrong
turn, and a body found by the rooks in the spring.

## The two things nobody quite claims are animals

Kept ambiguous. Do not confirm, do not stat, do not build an encounter around them.
Their function is to explain why Larenne does not cross the moor after dark, and to
sit at the same volume as the PC's own quiet wrongnesses.

**Fen-candles.** Pale lights over the wet ground beyond the peat-cuts, seen most
often in the still hours before rain. Any Breton hedge-wife will tell you frankly
that it is marsh-gas and nothing else, and she will say so while making the sign
of the Eight, because the other thing everyone knows is that the lights sometimes
travel with you at your own walking pace, and sometimes they are on the causeway
side, and sometimes a man who followed one is found in the sink come spring. Perhaps
twice a generation. Enough.

**The tallow hound.** A long low dog the colour of rendered fat, seen on the moor road
between the last hedgerow and the old manor's gate. Nobody in living memory has been
bitten by it. The belief is only that it walks beside you a while, keeps your pace,
does not breathe audibly, and turns off at the gate — and that whoever it walks with
buries someone before the year is out. Larenne treats this as superstition in daylight
and as settled fact at night, and both positions are held by the same people.

**A note on the old manor.** In late summer, moths gather on its shuttered windows in
numbers that people remark on and then stop remarking on. That is all. It is a detail
for later, not a creature.

# Culture — Larenne, and What He Carries Into It

## I. THE VISIBLE WORLD: BRETON LIFE IN A MOORLAND MARKET TOWN

### The shape of a day

Larenne keeps its hours by the Chapterhouse bells, and has for longer than anyone
troubles to say. There are four that matter. **Firstlight** is rung while the sky is
still the colour of dishwater, and it is less a summons to prayer than a permission
to make noise: after Firstlight a man may split wood, beat a rug, or shout at his
own children without being thought ill-bred. The **Trade Toll** comes mid-morning and
opens the market cross — before it, no bargain struck in Larenne is legally a
bargain, which is why so many of them are struck at a quarter to. The **Ash Bell** at
dusk is the one people actually love. It means hearths are banked, doors are shut,
and whatever you have not finished today has stopped being your fault. And there is
the **Long Bell**, which is rung slowly and only twice a year on average: for a death,
or for a coach gone into the fen. Everyone in Larenne can tell the Long Bell from
the others while still asleep.

The Hollow Horn runs on those bells too, though it runs on them backward. The inn
wakes at the Trade Toll and does its real business between the Ash Bell and
midnight, which is why the doorsman's life is lived in the hours the rest of the
town has agreed to stop existing in. This is worth saying plainly: he sees Larenne
from its threshold and at its worst hour, and Larenne sees him the same way. The
town's opinion of him and his opinion of the town were both formed after dark, by
tired people.

### Market day

Loredas is market day. The moor farms come in along the coaching road with
peat, wool, hedgerow apples, and mutton that has walked to town on its own feet, and
the town buys, sells, gossips, and litigates in a single continuous motion around
the **market cross** — a squat granite pillar with the Eight cut into its faces, so
worn now that only Zenithar's scales and Kynareth's bird are still legible, which
Larenne considers appropriate to its priorities.

Bargains are witnessed rather than written. The **Market Court** convenes at the cross
between the Trade Toll and the Ash Bell, and it is not a court in any sense the
Kingdom of Daggerfall would recognise: it is an old man with an extraordinary memory
who recites the terms back at both parties until neither will admit to having meant
anything else. When a bargain is closed, both parties flick a copper into the
nearest banked hearth. These are **ash-coppers**; the Chapterhouse sifts them out on
Sundas and calls it a tithe, and everyone permits this fiction because the
alternative is a written tax.

Market day food is eaten standing up. **Renne-cake** is the town's staple and its
minor point of pride — a coarse oatcake baked on a griddle stone and folded, still
too hot, around mutton dripping and a smear of heather honey. There is **moor-eel**
soused in vinegar and sold from a barrel, which visitors find alarming and which
Larenne children eat like sweets. There is small beer, cut with well-water, drunk
from a shared horn cup on a string; and there is the strong stuff, which the Horn's
regulars call **Direnni-water** because, they say, it takes an elf's constitution to
survive it. The joke is very old. It is told to every new coachman as though it were
new, and the coachmen have learned to laugh.

### The inn's own law

The Hollow Horn has customs older than its current innkeeper, and they are the
customs of a *coaching* inn — that is, an institution whose business is strangers.

**Hearth-price.** A traveller who comes in out of the rain sits at the fire for free
for as long as his cloak steams, and begins paying the moment it stops. No one
measures this. Everyone knows exactly when it has happened. It is the single most
Breton thing about the place: a genuine kindness with a precise expiry, publicly
observed.

**The hanging beam.** Blades come off at the door and go up on the beam, each with a
chalk mark. The doorsman is expected to remember every mark without consulting it,
and to hand each weapon back into the right hand on the way out, in the dark, to a
man who has been drinking for six hours. This is regarded as the actual skill of the
job. The stillness and the size are considered decoration.

**Named rooms.** The Horn does not number its rooms; it names them — the Cold Room,
the Widow's, the Nook, the Coachman's, and the one over the stable that has never
been called anything but Upstairs. A guest given the Widow's has been told something
about his standing without a word being said, and if he is Breton enough to know it,
he will pay more next time to avoid it.

**The backward cup.** When a regular dies, the next round in the common room is
poured with the hand turned over, wrist upward, and nobody drinks until the pouring
is done. It takes perhaps four seconds. It is the whole of the funeral, as far as
the Horn is concerned; the Chapterhouse can have the rest.

### Barony and commons

The Barony of Larenne governs by courtesy, which is to say by a series of customs
that would be intolerable if anyone called them laws. There is the **hearth-tax**,
counted by chimneys rather than heads, so that a crowded house pays what an empty
one does. There are the **road days** — three days a year when every household owes a
body to the coaching road's ditches and drains, and the Barony provides beer and a
priest, and the town works, and grumbles, and rather enjoys itself. And there is the
**table-summons**: the Baroness's right to call any townsman to answer questions at
her own table, over her own food, as a guest. Nobody has ever been arrested by a
table-summons. Nobody has ever refused one either.

The class line in Larenne is not drawn by wealth — the tanner is richer than two
knights — but by *whether you are expected to explain yourself*. Commons explain.
Nobility ask. The Chapterhouse is the hinge: it feeds the poor at the **Loredas dole**,
keeps the parish rolls, teaches letters to whichever children can be spared, and
patiently reminds the Barony of the Eight's opinion on matters where the Eight can
be got to agree with the Chapterhouse.

Magic here is domestic and unremarkable. A hedge-wife charms a sty off an eyelid for
two coppers; the Chapterhouse's restoration work is competent and free on high days;
and Bretons shrug off small curses the way they shrug off weather — *it slid off him
like a hedge-curse* is a compliment about a man's temper, not his blood. Larenne is
quietly proud of its Direnni-thinned ancestry in the way people are proud of a
great-grandmother's silver: unearned, unspent, unquestionably theirs.

### Sayings

*Peace-and-taxes* — the standard grumbled greeting, and the town's entire political
philosophy. *A Daggerfall answer* — one that is technically true and no use at all.
*Kept dry* — lucky. *May your cloak stop steaming* — either a blessing or an eviction,
entirely dependent on tone, and both meanings are always faintly present. *Give me
eight* — give me a moment (from counting the Divines). *Well-mannered as Larenne* —
what other towns say about it, and not entirely kindly.

### How a Breton town treats an Argonian doorsman

Not badly. This is the important and uncomfortable thing.

He is never refused service, never turned from a shop, never spat at in the street.
Larenne is far too well-mannered for that, and besides, it rather *likes* having him
— the Horn's Argonian is a civic possession, mentioned to visitors in the same
breath as the market cross and the good bridge. Cruelty here is not a fist. It is a
thousand small administrative and conversational facts, each individually
defensible.

He is always seated near the door, when he is seated at all, and no one remembers
deciding this. He draws his wash-water second, after the kitchen girls, which is not
a rule and has never been said aloud and has never once failed to happen. The
Chapterhouse's parish roll lists the town's souls by household and then, in a
separate hand at the bottom of the page, *beast-folk, resident, one* — a clerical
category some clerk in Daggerfall invented two centuries ago, carried forward
faithfully by every scribe since, meaning nothing personal by it. Brother Aumery has
lately taken to re-inking that line in a way that makes it harder to read, and has
not explained himself to anyone, including himself.

People compliment him by measuring him. *You speak beautifully* — said to a man who
has spoken Breton since before he can remember. *You're very gentle, for one of
yours.* *I always feel safe when you're on the door* — which is true, and is also a
way of saying that a thing on the door is a thing that has a place. Drunks and small
children occasionally call him *it*, and the room corrects them, briskly, every time
— not for his comfort but for its own, because Larenne does not care to think of
itself as the kind of town where that happens. He has learned to be grateful for the
correction anyway. Gratitude is cheaper than the alternative.

There is real warmth in it too, and that is what makes it hard to be angry at. The
tanner's wife leaves warm renne-cake on the doorpost on the coldest nights and has
never in nine years used his name. The Market Court's rhymer greets him with an
elaborate, perfect courtesy he extends to no other servant in town, and it is
courtesy, and it is also a fence, and both facts are true at once. The children have
a skipping rhyme about fen-lizards — *snap-jaw, snap-jaw, count your teeth* — which
they sing near him with no malice whatsoever, which is worse than malice, and which
he has never mentioned.

He is known. He is not known *by name* so much as by function. In a town where
belonging is a performance of manners, he performs very well, and everyone agrees he
belongs, and no one would be able to say what he thinks about anything.

## II. THE SUBMERGED WORLD: WHAT AN ARGONIAN CARRIES OUT OF BLACK MARSH

### The Hist

Everything Larenne believes about Argonians is wrong in an interesting way, because
Larenne believes Argonians are a people, and Argonians are — but they are a people
in the way a hand is a hand: attached to something.

In Black Marsh there are the Hist: trees, if one insists on the word, though they
are trees the way a river is water. The villages of the Saxhleel are built at their
roots. Hatchlings are brought to the root-pools and drink the sap, and the sap
shapes them — body and temper and something under temper — and this is not
metaphor, and it is not magic in any sense a Breton chapel or a Direnni text would
recognise. It is simply how Argonians are made. A hatchling's first year is said to
belong to the Hist and only afterward to its parents. Argonians say this the way
Bretons say a child is born under a Divine: as a fact so ordinary it is boring.

The Hist remember. That is the second thing, and the harder one. What one Argonian
knows, the tree may hold; what the tree holds, another may be given, generations
later, without ever having been told. Argonians do not find this eerie. They find
the Breton arrangement eerie — all those separate skulls, each one losing everything
it held the moment it stopped.

### Hatch-kin

Kinship in the Marsh runs sideways, not down. Those hatched in the same clutch, in
the same season, at the same root, are **hatch-kin**, and that bond outranks parentage
in nearly every way that matters: hatch-kin share names, obligations, debts, and
sometimes a single reputation among them. Parents are real and loved and secondary.

This is why the Breton question *who were your people?* lands wrong on an Argonian
ear. It is not a rude question. It is a badly-built one, like asking a man which of
his legs he walks with. The Marsh question is *which pool, which season, which
tree* — and an Argonian who cannot answer it is not embarrassed so much as
structurally incomplete, in a way that has no Cyrodilic word.

Names follow the same logic. An Argonian's Tamrielic name is a translation, usually
of a deed or a role or a thing the Hist noticed, and it is expected to change over a
life as the person does. A name that never changes means a life that never turned.
Many Argonians beyond the Marsh carry a **dry-name** — the name outsiders gave them,
or the mistranslation that stuck at a border post — and keep the **wet-name** private,
or have lost it, or were never given one at all.

### The green murmur

Those who have it do not talk about it, and this is not mystery-mongering; it is
that the vocabulary does not exist in any language Larenne speaks. The nearest an
Argonian will usually come is to say the green murmur is like a room you have left
the door open on. You are not in it. You can feel the draught.

Close to the Marsh it is a constant, low, companionable pressure — a sense of being
attended to. Far from it, it thins toward nothing, and what remains is peculiar and
easy to dismiss: a taste at the back of the mouth in early spring. A dream of
standing water and enormous roots, recurring, oddly specific, in a person who has
never seen either. An unwillingness to burn certain woods, with no reason offered.
An occasional flat certainty about something that cannot possibly be known. Argonians
in the diaspora tend to file all of this under homesickness, because homesickness is
a thing Bretons will accept as an explanation, and the truth would require an hour
and would not be believed at the end of it.

It is worth saying that Hist-communion is serious and half-alien and entirely real.
It is not superstition dressed up. It simply operates on assumptions — about memory,
about individuality, about what a life is for — that no chapel of the Eight has any
apparatus for holding.

### Root-cut

There is a Jel word for an Argonian raised away from the Hist, and the usual
Cyrodilic rendering is **root-cut**. In Jel it is descriptive and carries pity but no
insult. In Cyrodilic it cannot help sounding like an insult, and so Argonians in the
west generally do not translate it at all.

They come west for the ordinary reasons: the Dunmer slave-takers who worked the
Marsh's northern edge for centuries and sold what they took; debts; ships; the
Empire's roads, which go everywhere and are therefore used by everyone. Whatever the
route, the experience at the far end is the same. The root-cut keep small private
observances that they would find difficult to justify if asked. A piece of wood
carried in a pocket, unremarkable, which no one else may touch. A window left open
in spring regardless of the weather. Counting — in a language half-remembered, under
the breath, to a clutch-number that means nothing to anyone else — when frightened.
None of this is doctrine. It is what is left when the tree is four hundred miles
away and the practice has decayed into habit.

The absence expresses itself most sharply at Breton funerals. Argonians do not bury.
The dead go back to the Hist; there is nothing to put in a hole and no reason to
stand around a hole while it is filled. So an Argonian at a Chapterhouse burial in
Larenne, on a wet Loredas, among people being decent to him, finds that he has
nothing whatever to do with his hands — and that this, more than the rhymes or the
second bucket or *beast-folk, resident, one*, is the moment the distance becomes
unbearable and impossible to explain to a single person present.

And there is one more thing about the marked and the root-cut both, which nobody in
Larenne has any framework for and which the Argonians themselves state without
elaboration: the Hist do not mark a hatchling idly, and the Hist are patient, and a
thing set aside is not a thing forgotten. It is simply a thing that has not been
asked for yet.

# Factions — Who Holds What In Larenne

Larenne has perhaps four hundred hearths, a market charter, and one road worth
mentioning. Nobody in it is powerful. What people have instead is standing — a
position in a web of debts, favours, tithes, and ninety-year grudges that everyone
can recite and nobody wrote down. A doorsman at the Hollow Horn sees more of that
web in a week than the Baroness sees in a year, because all of it eventually walks
past him to get a drink.

Two things should be true of every faction scene in this campaign. First: the
stakes are real to the people in them and small to anyone else, and the narration
should take the people's side, not the outsider's. Second: the PC is inside this
web without being a member of it. He is the Argonian at the Horn. Everyone knows
where he stands at the door; nobody quite knows where he stands.

## The Hollow Horn

The Horn is not a tavern. It is a licensed change-house on the Daggerfall coaching
road, which means it holds the **post-right**: the coach stops here, the teams are
swapped here, the mail bag is signed for here, and every passenger who wants a bed
between Daggerfall and the Wrothgarian road takes one under this roof or sleeps in
the coach. The taproom is the smaller half of the business. The stableyard is the
larger. The post-right is the whole of it — without the right, the Horn is a
moorland alehouse with eleven beds it cannot fill.

**Mistress Perrine Aubrec** has held the Horn for nineteen years, since her husband
Gerauld died of a chest complaint in a wet spring. She is somewhere past fifty, very
short, and has the flat unhurried competence of a woman who has never once in her
life been surprised by an emergency. She keeps the house ledger in a private
shorthand of her own devising — hooks and bars, no words — and she is entirely
aware that this makes her the only person alive who knows what the Horn is worth.
She hired the PC herself, off the yard, over the objections of two aldermen and her
own niece, and has never explained why beyond *he was standing still and everyone
else was shouting*. She does not praise him. She has also never once docked his
wage for a broken bench, and both of them understand that this is the same thing.

**Toussaint Merigot**, called Touss, is head ostler and has been in that yard forty
years under three owners. He is deaf in the left ear and answers questions he has
half-heard with whatever he wanted to say anyway. He can name a coach team by the
sound of its gait through the arch before it is in the yard, and does, aloud, to
nobody. Touss is the one man in Larenne who has never in the PC's hearing referred
to him as *the Argonian* — not out of principle, but because he sorts people the
way he sorts horses, by whether they are any use, and has long since filed the PC
under *use*.

**Nolwenn Aubrec** is Perrine's niece, thirty-one, and runs the kitchen, the linen,
and as much of the accounting as her aunt permits — which is the taproom slate and
nothing else. Nolwenn expects to inherit the Horn. She is not certain she will.
Perrine has never said so, has a living brother in Wayrest with sons, and enjoys not
saying so more than is entirely kind. This is the quiet ache underneath every one of
Nolwenn's small tyrannies about the pantry, and it makes her sharp with anyone her
aunt visibly favours, the PC included. She is not cruel. She is simply nineteen years
into a job that might turn out to have been somebody else's all along.

**Ambrose Cathelin**, the pot-boy, is fourteen, called Broz, and hero-worships the
PC with the total absence of tact peculiar to that age. He asks about Black Marsh
constantly — about the trees, about eggs, about whether it is true they eat mud —
and the PC does not have the answers, which Broz has never noticed, because the PC
has become very good at giving him a story instead of a fact.

The Horn's regulars are its other half. **Dame Marguerite Osse**, the Widow Osse,
takes the corner settle every market day and has done for twenty-two years; she is
the town's chief wool-factor and the actual price of fleece in Larenne is whatever
she says it is over a cup of hot ale between the eighth and ninth hour. **Hanryk
Sowe** is a Nord, drives the Daggerfall run, arrives every ninth day with the news,
the mail, and a running tab that Perrine allows to grow to exactly forty drakes and
never one more. **Ysore Cabray** sells charms out of the snug — a hedge-wife of the
usual Glenumbra sort, Direnni-descended if you believe her mother, and she keeps the
Horn's cold-larder charm renewed twice a year for the price of her seat.

The Horn's live trouble is this: **Guiot Malebranche**, who keeps the Blue Mare at
Trevec Cross on the far side of the moor, has petitioned the barony to move the
post-right to his house, on the grounds that the moor road floods below Larenne four
weeks in the year and the coach has twice been turned back. It is not a bad
argument. That is what frightens Perrine about it.

## The Barony of Larenne

The barony is sworn to the Crown of Daggerfall and hears from it about as often as
the weather changes — which is to say constantly and without much consequence, until
suddenly with a great deal of consequence.

**Baroness Isabeau Vaudry** holds Larenne in her own right, her husband Ottevin
having been dead eleven years. She is fifty-four, wears mourning grey out of habit
rather than grief, and has the disconcerting Vaudry manner of asking a question as
though she expects a true answer — including of stablehands, including of Argonians,
which unnerves people far more than contempt would. She is neither kind nor unkind.
She is a woman with a ledger problem.

The ledger problem is the **hearth-roll**. Every twelfth year the Daggerfall
exchequer counts the barony's hearths and sets its dues by the count. Larenne's last
roll was taken in the year after the flood, when three lanes stood empty and half the
carters were wintering elsewhere, and the town has been paying on that undercount ever
since — legally, blamelessly, and with enormous relief. The new roll is due. An honest
count would raise Larenne's dues by something near a third, and the Baroness has
exactly one place to find a third: the market tolls. Which is to say from the
wool-factors, the stallholders, and the post-right at the Hollow Horn.

So the whole town is now engaged in a shared, low-grade, entirely mundane conspiracy
about arithmetic. Is a stable loft a hearth if a man sleeps in it? Are the Horn's
eleven letting-rooms hearths, or one hearth? Is the almshouse a hearth or a charity?
Nobody will lie outright. Everybody will be extremely helpful about definitions.
**Serjeant-Assessor Guiscard Thenn** has been sent from Daggerfall to settle it, and
he is lodging — because there is nowhere else — at the Hollow Horn, where the
doorsman takes his coat every evening and where he has already begun asking mild,
patient, extremely well-aimed questions of the staff.

The succession is the barony's other unfinished sentence. **Amaury Vaudry**, the son,
is twenty-three, likeable, an adequate rider, and wants very badly to be somewhere
that is not Larenne — Daggerfall, a knightly order, anywhere with a name. **Sibylle
Vaudry**, nineteen, has read the moor-law, understands the turbary rolls better than
the reeve does, and is the one the tenants go to when they want a thing actually
fixed. Breton custom favours Amaury. Everyone in the household knows what everyone in
the household knows, and nobody says it, and it sits at the Vaudry table every
evening like a fourth chair.

Outward, the barony has two faces. **Reeve Osmond Cabray** collects the tolls, keeps
the market peace, and is cousin to the hedge-wife in the Horn's snug; he is tired,
broadly fair, and can be relied upon to let the Horn's benches spill onto the square
on fair days in exchange for a keg finding its way to his kitchen, an arrangement so
old it has stopped being corruption and become custom. **Serjeant Aloys Bec**
commands the town watch, which is four men, two of them his cousins, and a bell. Bec's
private policy — never spoken, never written, never acknowledged — is that trouble at
the Hollow Horn is handled by the Hollow Horn's doorsman, and that this is best for
everyone. He has bought the PC a drink for this exactly once, and did not say what it
was for.

Beyond the town wall, **Ser Ranulf Coudrec of Wethermarch** holds a knight's fee on
the moor's western edge and has been arguing with the Vaudrys about peat since before
either family's current generation was born. The dispute is the turbary rights on
Cullen's Wet: whose tenants may cut, how many spades' breadth, and in which weeks.
Ninety years of it. It has produced perhaps six bloodied noses in the Horn's yard on
market days, one broken cart, and a truly extraordinary quantity of documentation.
Coudrec has lately begun hinting that he might take it over the Baroness's head to a
Daggerfall court, which is the single most aggressive thing anyone in this parish has
done in a decade.

---

## The Larenne Chapterhouse

A minor house of the Eight — the Nine, officially, since the new altar went up, and
the older half of Larenne still says Eight and will go on saying it until they die.
The chapterhouse's real business is not devotion. It is the almshouse, the infirmary,
the burial ground, and the records: births, deaths, tithes, wills, and the parish's
memory of itself.

**Prior Faustin Belloc** runs it, is past seventy, and has narrowed his personal
religion down over the years to Stendarr and the almshouse loaves, which he considers
the same subject. He is the Baroness's chaplain by courtesy and her creditor by
accident — the chapterhouse has been carrying the barony's tithe arrears for two years
without mentioning it, and the Prior's great ambition is to convert that unmentioned
debt into a Julianos scriptorium before he dies. This puts him directly in the path
of the hearth-roll, and he knows it.

**Sister Ysolt Meaune** keeps the infirmary and is the nearest thing Larenne has to a
physician: restoration for what restoration answers, and vinegar, splints and honest
opinion for everything else. She has patched the PC's knuckles perhaps a dozen times.
She has also written in her day-book, in the ordinary way she notes anything, that the
Argonian at the Horn does not startle — not that he is brave, that he does not startle,
which is a different observation — and she has not thought about it since.

**Brother Aumery** is the chapterhouse's chronicler and its least dignified asset: a
scribe of no rank and boundless curiosity who illustrates the town's notable happenings
in the margins of ledgers that are supposed to contain grain accounts, and who cannot
stop himself adding captions. The Prior has told him to stop. The Prior has also
quietly kept every page.

# Geography — Larenne and the Larenne Moor

## The shape of the country

Glenumbra is wet country. The sea-fog comes in off the Iliac Bay in the small
hours and does not always leave, and between the fog and the rain there is a
long season in which the moor is less a place than a condition. Larenne sits in
the middle of it, four days' coach south of nothing much and three days' coach
north of the same, on the road that runs between Daggerfall and Camlorn. Both
cities are real to Larenne chiefly as directions. A man says *he went down to
Daggerfall* the way he might say *he went into the fog*, meaning only that he
is no longer here.

The town is not old by the standards of High Rock, which is to say it is only
about eight hundred years old. It exists because the moor road needed somewhere
to change horses at that particular distance, and because the Anwyl — a brown,
peat-stained, unhurried little water — could be forded there and later bridged.
Everything Larenne is grew out of those two facts. It has a market because the
road brought buyers. It has a chapterhouse because the market brought coin and
coin wants blessing. It has a barony because somebody had to weigh the wool.

## The town

The heart of Larenne is **the Weighing Yard**, which strangers call the market
square and no local ever does. It is cobbled in the middle and mud at the edges,
and at its north end stands the barony's public wool-beam under a slate roof on
four posts — a set of scales as tall as a man, chained, with the Vaudry mark
punched into the brass. Every fleece sold in Larenne is supposed to cross that
beam and pay its fraction. Many do.

The Yard's four sides are, by long habit: the beam and **the Hollow Horn** to
the north; **Vaudry Hall** to the east, a fortified manor house rather than a
castle, with arrow-slits nobody has needed in three hundred years and a good
kitchen garden; **the Larenne Chapterhouse** to the south, whose bell — called
the Matron, though no one remembers why — rings the hours, the high days, and
also, by an arrangement that scandalized a previous prior, the arrival of the
southbound coach; and to the west a row of merchant frontages that changes hands
often enough that nobody bothers naming it.

The Horn itself fronts the Yard with a wide carriage arch through to its coach
yard, and that arch is the most important twenty feet of ground in this story,
because it is where the door is worked. From under it you can see the whole
square, the chapterhouse steps, the road coming in past Peatgate, and who is
waiting for whom.

Out from the Yard run the lanes that do the actual work:

- **Tenter Row**, west and downhill, where the fleeces are washed, fulled and
  stretched on hooked frames. It smells of lanolin and stale piss in all weather
  and everyone pretends it does not. **Coudray's Mill** stands at the bottom of
  it on the Anwyl, and the mill's undershot wheel is the sound Larenne sleeps to.
- **Peatgate**, north, which is not a gate and has not been since the wall came
  down for building stone. There is an arch, a toll-post, and a shed. Everything
  cut from the moor comes in this way and pays a copper the cartload.
- **The Bellows**, east along the road out, where the farriers, wheelwrights and
  the one blacksmith cluster because coaches break and horses throw shoes and
  the trade follows the traffic.
- **Sallow Lane**, behind the Row — cheap lodgings, damp walls, and where most
  of Larenne's handful of non-Bretons sleep. The town's tolerance is genuine,
  ordinary, and it lives on Sallow Lane rather than on the Yard.
- **The Pack Bridge**, three arches, too narrow for two carts, the subject of
  perhaps a quarter of all litigation heard in Vaudry Hall.

Market is Loredas. The great wool fair is in Hearthfire and doubles the town for
a week.

## The moor

**The Larenne Moor** is heather, cotton-grass, bog myrtle, hidden water and
sheep. It is crossed by the **Coach Road** — properly the Bay Road — and by
older ways that predate it and go where they please.

Landmarks, in the order a traveller meets them going south from Peatgate:

**The Black Cuts**, the peat workings, where each household holds a hereditary
turf-plot with a name of its own (Sourbank, the Widow's Room, Gaultier's Long
Cut). Cutting starts after First Seed and the whole town smells of turf smoke
from Frostfall on.

**The Marchstone**, a weathered grey pillar of Direnni work standing crooked in
open ground, its carving worn to suggestion. It marks a boundary nobody now
observes. Sheep scratch against it. Children are told not to sleep in its shadow,
and are not told why, because the reason has been lost and only the telling
survives.

**The Sisters**, a stand of old wind-crippled thorn and oak in a fold of the
ground, the only real trees for miles. The chapterhouse maintains there are nine
of them, this being a decent number. Nobody who has counted has ever got nine.

**The Widow's Stone**, at the sixth milestone: a niched wayside marker where
coachmen leave a copper against foul weather. The chapterhouse calls it a shrine
of Kynareth. The drivers call it the Widow's Stone. Both are correct in their
fashion and neither party presses the point.

**The Drown**, off the road to the west — a soft place in the moss that takes
sheep and, twice in living memory, men. It is not in the same place it was in
the last generation's stories, which is the honest and unsettling part.

**The Hare's Road** is not on any map. It leaves the Anwyl below Coudray's Mill,
goes round the back of the moor by dry ridges, and rejoins the Bay Road well
south of the Peatgate toll-post. Wool that has not crossed the wool-beam travels
it, and so does brandy that has not paid its duty. Everyone knows. It is, in
Larenne's view, less a crime than a form of weather.

South, a long day's drive, is **Malmont Cross**, the next change-house, where
the moor gives out into hedge and orchard country and the road bears away for
Daggerfall. North, over a toll-bridge, is **Stanchgate**, and beyond that the
road to Camlorn.

## The Dower House

Two miles off the Bay Road, up an avenue of limes, stands the Vaudry family's
own **Dower House**. It is not a stranger's manor — it is the Baroness's own,
shut up eighteen years ago the season her elder brother Jocelin Vaudry
renounced his claim and went down to Daggerfall to take orders. Letters came
for a year and then stopped. The house has stood shuttered ever since — the
chapterhouse's copy of the deed is incomplete, a transfer with a signature and
no named grantee, one of the documents Brother Aumery would very much like an
afternoon with.

The facts, such as Larenne has them, are these. No smoke from the chimneys. No
lights. No servants seen in the town, no orders placed with any Larenne merchant,
no tithe paid and none demanded. And yet the avenue is kept clear, the gates are
kept sound, the deer park wall is mended where it falls, and every year at
Evening Star a factor comes up from Daggerfall and pays the barony's land-dues
to the penny, in coin that is always a little older than the coin in general
circulation. He signs, he takes his receipt, he goes.

The shepherds put their flocks elsewhere. Not from fear, they will tell you —
the grazing is simply poor up there, and the dogs won't work well, and it is a
long way back in the dark. The moor road runs past the gate-lodge and everybody
who walks it walks a little faster on that stretch, and would be embarrassed to
be asked why.

# The History of Larenne

## What the Name Means, Depending on Whom You Ask

Larenne has two histories, and they do not like each other.

The chapterhouse keeps the first one in a bound assize roll with a cracked spine, and it says that the name is *Lar-Renne*, which is elvish, or was, and means the Lady's Rest — that in the days when the Direnni held High Rock from their island in the bay, some highborn Aldmeri woman broke her journey here and found the place restful. Brother Aumery will show you the line if you let him. He will also, if pressed, admit that the hand that wrote it wrote it four hundred years after the fact, and that the man who wrote it was being paid by a baron who wanted an elvish pedigree for a town that had none.

The drovers keep the second history, which is not written anywhere, and it says that a *laren* is simply the old moor-word for the firm place — the gravel shelf where the cart stops sinking. There are peat bogs to the north and peat bogs to the south and between them a mile and a half of hard ground, and that is the whole reason there is a town here. Not a battle. Not a saint. Just the only dry place to stop.

Both stories are told at the Hollow Horn most nights, usually by the same people, and the version a man tells depends on how much he has drunk and whom he is trying to impress.

## The Old Wars, Which Happened Somewhere Else

Larenne claims a battle. Everyone in Glenumbra claims that battle. Somewhere out on these moors, in the First Era, the Direnni broke an Alessian host — the story is real enough, the ground is disputed, and every parish between here and Camlorn has a hummock it swears is the grave-mound. Larenne's is out past Ninebank, and the peat-cutters turn up iron from it, and the iron is almost always a horseshoe. Once in a generation it is something better, and then it goes to the chapterhouse and is called elf-iron by people who have never seen elf-iron.

What the town actually remembers, in its bones, is not the First Era. It is the long Second Era gap when there was no Empire and no law on the road, and the drove-trade went armed or did not go at all. That is when the horn was hung.

## The Horn

The Hollow Horn is named for the thing over its door: a great ox-horn, hollowed, banded in tarnished silver, cracked from lip to tip so that it will not sound. In the bad centuries a watchman kept it on the roof-ridge and blew it when the road came up clear — one long note meaning the drove could move, no note at all meaning stay behind the wall and say nothing. It has not been sounded in three hundred years, because it cannot be, and because nobody has wanted to. There is a joke about it in Larenne, worn smooth with use, which is that if you ever do hear the Horn, you should not stop to ask who blew it. People laugh. They laugh a little less at three in the morning.

The same horn is also, legally, the town's ale measure. The Kingdom of Daggerfall's market charter — early in the Peace, when the Septim line was mending roads and selling charters to pay for it — set Larenne's lawful pint by the capacity of a horn-measure kept at the sign of the Horn, and the innkeeper is technically an officer of the market on that account, and gets a shilling a year for it, and has been getting the same shilling since the coin was worth something.

## How the Kingdom Touches Larenne (Barely)

Larenne is a barony sworn to Daggerfall. In practice this means three things, and only three.

It means the levy, which was commuted to coin four generations back — so many pounds a year in place of so many men-at-arms, a bargain that seemed shrewd when it was struck and now merely seems old. It means the assize, a royal justiciar who rides the circuit every third autumn, sits two days in the chapterhouse hall, hears whatever the barony has not managed to settle, and rules on fences, wells, bastardy and peat. And it means the wool contract, which is not the Kingdom's business at all but which everyone treats as though it were, because the wool goes to Daggerfall and the price comes back from Daggerfall and there is no arguing with either.

There is also, technically, a boundary dispute. The moor-edge north of Ninebank has been *under review* between Larenne and its neighbours for something over eighty years, which means the bridge-toll there has not been lawfully collected in living memory, which means it is collected anyway by whoever gets to the bridge first. Nobody wants it resolved. A resolved dispute would have an owner, and an owner would have to pay for the bridge.

Beyond that: the Empire is a thing that happens to other places. A legion detachment came through once, on its way somewhere it did not explain, and bought every barrel in town and paid fairly, and that is the most the Emperor has ever done for Larenne. The Peace is a word people use with a flat little irony, the way you speak of a landlord who has not yet come to look at the roof.

## The Vaudrys, and the Thing Everyone Knows About Them

The Vaudrys were not always the barony. Three generations back they were wool-factors — buyers, weighers, men with scales — and they married into the title, and the two or three old families in Larenne have never entirely stopped calling them the wool-lords, though only among themselves and only after the second cup.

Then there was the annulment. Baron Alric Vaudry married Ysabeau Corvenne of Daggerfall for her father's money, spent a good portion of it, and had the marriage undone some three years later on a point of consanguinity certified — after long and prayerful consideration — by the Larenne chapterhouse, which acquired a new lead roof that same summer. The roof is still on. So is Ysabeau Corvenne. She never went home. She lives in the tall house on Market Row, comes to the Hollow Horn on the first of each month, takes one glass of Wayrest wine, and sits at the window from which one can see the barony's gate. She has done this for something near forty years. The Corvennes still hold the wool contract, and Larenne's wool still fetches a little less than Camlorn's, and nobody in town believes for one moment that those two facts are unrelated.

The present Baroness Vaudry came to the seat sideways. She was the second child. Her elder brother Jocelin renounced his claim and went to Daggerfall to take orders, or to take something, and was not seen again; letters came for a year and then stopped. The Vaudry dower-house out on the moor road — the Dower House, everyone calls it, as though there were only one — was shut up that same season and has been shut ever since. The shutters get repainted. Nobody is quite sure who pays for that.

## The Years People Count By

Ask anyone in Larenne how long ago a thing happened and they will not give you a year. They will give you one of these.

**The Wet Years.** Three seasons of rain, close to forty years gone. The oats drowned in the ground, the sheep took the water-rot, and the barony opened its granary — and charged for it, at a price that was not quite robbery and was very much not charity. Grandmothers still say *at granary prices* to mean a favour that came with a bill. It is the single deepest grudge in the town and it is nobody's fault living, which makes it worse.

**The Malt Fire.** Some twenty-five years back, a fire started in the brewhouse behind Market Row on a dry night with a north wind and took a third of the row before the bucket-line stopped it. Two dead, both of them in the row and neither of them named in the telling any more. The barony's ordinance afterward required brick on the market side, which is why one flank of the square is brick and hard-edged and the other is old leaning timber, and why the Hollow Horn's north wing does not match the rest of the Hollow Horn.

And the blame fell on a Khajiit sugar-tinker who had pitched by the brewhouse and moved on two days before it happened. He was never found and never charged and it has never mattered; the caravans understood the shape of it and stopped coming, and Larenne has been a town that outsiders pass through rather than trade in ever since. The town does not think of itself as having done anything. That is precisely the point. There are perhaps four people alive who could tell you about a brewer's apprentice, a lamp, and a spilled tun, and none of them ever has.

**The Ninebank Judgment.** Twenty-odd years ago the assize ruled on the turbary — the peat-cutting rights — at Ninebank, between the Perrenots and the Gascels, and the ruling exists, in ink, in oilcloth, and is produced annually. It has never once been obeyed. Both families cut the same bank on the first day of cutting, every year, and there is a fight, and the fight has become so exact in its steps — the same shouting, the same shoving, the same one broken tool — that the town turns out to watch it the way it turns out for a feast day. The Kingdom considers the matter settled. Larenne considers it a tradition.

**The Ninebank Man.** Nine years ago the cutters brought up a body out of the deep peat: a man, tanned brown as saddle-leather, hair still on him, a twisted cord still around his throat. Older than the Empire, the chapterhouse said, and probably older than that. Brother Aumery drew him and buried him in the chapterhouse yard with the Eight named over him and the ninth niche's god named too, quietly, at the end, because one does not know what such a man would have wanted. What the bog teaches Larenne, and what Larenne would rather not have been taught, is that this ground does not let things rot. It keeps them. It keeps them whole, and it gives them back on its own schedule, and the giving back is never asked for.

## The Argonian at the Horn

There has been one other Argonian in Larenne's living memory: a bargeman who worked a season on the coast road and went south again before the frost, and whose name three different people will give you three different versions of. Then there is the doorsman at the Hollow Horn, who has been there long enough that the market children do not stare, long enough to be owed money by half the regulars, long enough to be *the Argonian at the Horn* — which is a place in the town, and a warm one, and is not the same thing as being from here.

Larenne's history with outsiders is not cruel. It is worse than cruel; it is *conditional*. The town remembers the Malt Fire without ever saying what it remembers about it, and the memory sits under the floor of every kindness the town extends, the way the peat sits under the gravel shelf, holding whatever it was given, waiting to be cut.

# Magic in Larenne — The Unquiet Peace

## 1. Hedge-work: magic as a trade, not a wonder

In Glenumbra, magic is a service one purchases, like thatching or shoeing. Bretons
are the descendants of Direnni elves and Nedic farmers, and their part of that
inheritance shows up less as glory than as competence: half the households on the
Larenne moor own at least one charm they did not make themselves, and know
roughly what it cost.

The town's practitioner is Ysolt Fauvel, called the charm-wife, who keeps the
Rushlight House at the moor edge past Cordwain Row. Her work is a ledger of small
necessities. Damp-wards cut into the door-lintels of the wool sheds, because the
moor fog gets into fleece and rots a season's profit in a fortnight. A setting-charm
for a broken arm, which does not knit the bone but stops the swelling long enough
for the bone to be set properly by hand. Sheep-finding, at a rate per head. A rune
scratched under the Hollow Horn's ale-cellar shelf that keeps the cool in and which
the innkeeper renews every second Sun's Dusk, grumbling about the price. This is
Restoration and Alteration, the two respectable schools, taught the hedge way —
by imitation, by drill, by a decade of standing in someone's kitchen doing it wrong.

Two things are said of Breton magic that are worth saying plainly. The first is that
Bretons shrug off hedge-curses the way other folk shrug off weather; a bad charm
thrown at a Larenne carter will usually simply fail to bite, and everyone knows it,
which is why Larenne is a town where people threaten each other with the law
rather than with witchcraft. This protection is a Breton inheritance, in the blood,
and it does not extend to the Argonian on the Hollow Horn's door. He has no such
armor. Nobody has told him so, because to a Breton it is not a fact worth
mentioning, the way one does not mention that water is wet.

The second is that the Larenne Chapterhouse offers Restoration on high days and
for the poor, dispensed by the resident divine of Stendarr with the same brisk
charity as bread. Temple healing here is unremarkable and slightly rationed.
Brother Aumery, who keeps the chapterhouse ledgers and cannot stop drawing in
their margins, records every dispensation, and considers this the least interesting
part of his work.

Hedge-teaching has its own vocabulary, and the GM should use it rather than
game-words. A practitioner's capacity is *the draw* — how much a body can pull
before the hands shake and the vision silvers at the edges. A spell as taught is
*a figure*, meaning both its shape and its sequence of steps. To practice a figure
without spending any draw is *reciting cold*, which is what an honest teacher makes
an apprentice do for months before letting them spend a single measure. And every
figure has two halves, the making and *the closing* — and the closing is the half
that amateurs skip.

## 2. The Veiling Arts, and why nobody in Larenne will teach them

Illusion, in the hedge tradition, is called the Veiling Arts, and it has a bad name in
High Rock for the obvious reason: it is the school of the man who was not there.
Alteration moves the world; Restoration mends it; Illusion lies to the people in it.
A charm-wife who takes Illusion coin loses her Restoration custom, because nobody
will let a known veiler set their daughter's arm. Ysolt Fauvel knows the first two
figures and has not spoken them aloud in twenty years.

The tradition names its progression as five figures, and every practitioner from
Wayrest to Sentinel knows the ladder even if they can only climb the first rung:

- **The Candle** — a false appearance, one sense, one observer, held briefly. The
  standard drill is to make a lit candle look unlit to a single watcher across a table.
  Harmless, tedious, and the foundation of everything above it.
- **The Muffle** — the quieting of sound around a body. Coach-guards use it to move
  a spooked team past a bandit camp. Burglars use it for the other obvious thing.
- **The Veil** — true invisibility, brief and fragile, broken by a hard footfall, a
  shout, or a hand laid on another person. Expensive in draw. Universally
  understood to be the tool of thieves, spies, and worse.
- **The Sway** — the family of figures that work on the mind rather than the eye:
  calm, fear, the small persuasive glamour that makes a stranger's face read as
  familiar. Road-wardens use Calm on horses and swear that is all they use it on.
- **The Still Hand** — paralysis. The stopping of a body in place while its owner
  remains inside it, awake. There is no honest trade that requires the Still Hand.
  It is not taught in Glenumbra. It is taught, when it is taught, by people who want
  something in exchange, and the exchange is never money.

**The sticking.** The reason Illusion frightens people here is not the lying. It is
what happens when an untaught practitioner cannot perform the closing. A figure
that will not close does not simply fail; it settles onto the one who made it. This is
called *a sticking*, and it is the folk-horror engine of magic in this campaign. A
stuck Muffle leaves the caster deaf — first for hours, then for days, and if repeated,
for good. A stuck Veil leaves parts of a body out of step with the light: a hand that
arrives a half-beat after the arm, a reflection that turns its head a moment late. A
stuck Sway leaves the caster carrying the emotion themselves — three days of a
dread with no object, or a placid calm while the barn burns. A stuck Still Hand
locks a jaw, a finger, a knee, and it does not always come back. Stickings are not
usually lethal. They are permanent, visible, and they make a person into a story the
town tells. That is worse, in Larenne, than a wound.

## 3. Where an ordinary doorsman would actually start

There is no Mages Guild in Larenne. The nearest halls are in Daggerfall and
Wayrest, both of which require a sponsor, a fee, and a letter, and none of which
have ever admitted an Argonian doorsman from a coaching inn. So the path in
Larenne runs through three doors, all of them narrow, and one of them badly hung.

**The bad book.** Perrin Guisse keeps a copying-room off the chapterhouse yard,
where he transcribes contracts, wills, and — for a few coppers — chapbooks for
people who cannot afford real ones. Among his stock is *The Wardrobe of Mist,
being a Discourse upon Illusion for Persons of Modest Ability*, attributed to
Magister Ilaine Corbeau of Wayrest. Perrin's exemplar was copied from a copy. It is
missing the sixth chapter. The sixth chapter is the one on the closing. A determined
reader can absolutely get the Candle out of it, and probably the Muffle, and this is
precisely the danger: the book works well enough to be believed. Perrin himself is
deaf in the left ear and hears his own voice as though through a wall, and has been
since he was nineteen, and he will tell anyone who asks that this is what the moor
damp does to a man. It is not.

**The charm-wife.** Ysolt Fauvel will not teach the Veiling Arts. She will teach the
part underneath them — the draw, reciting cold, and above all the closing — because
she would rather train a fool than bury one, and because a doorsman who reads
faces all night is useful to her. Her price is not coin. She wants to know who comes
through the Hollow Horn's door and when: names, coaches, which travelers ask
directions to the moor road. She has her reasons and will not give them. Paying her
means quietly trading on the inn's trust, which is the PC's only real standing in this
town, and that is exactly the point of the price.

**The road-trade.** Four or five times a season a Hammerfell coachwarden named
Hazruun of Tigonus overnights in the Hollow Horn's yard on the Daggerfall–Wayrest
run. She is a Redguard who practices magic, which among her own people is a small
disgrace she has stopped explaining. She learned the Veiling Arts as tools, from
guards, for the road: the Muffle to move a team past trouble, the Sway to settle
horses, the Veil to sit still in a hedge for six hours while men with knives walk past.
She teaches nothing to anyone who has not ridden with her, because she does not
believe a figure is learned until it has been used with something at stake. Riding
with her means leaving Larenne, leaving the door, and taking a road on which
things genuinely happen. She is also, though she will not say so, riding that
particular stretch on purpose: a coach she warded went out onto the moor road
three winters ago and was never found, not the horses, not the wheels, not the
mud-marks, and she has been coming back ever since.

**The locked shelf.** Above Larenne's Wool Hall, the Direnni Cousinry of Glenumbra
meets four times a year — a subscription society of minor gentry who pay dues to be
told they have elven ancestors. Most of it is claret and genealogy. But the Cousinry
owns a locked press of copied texts, and on the second shelf are three volumes of
genuine Direnni-descended Illusion practice, including a complete Corbeau with the
sixth chapter intact. Aumeric Delance, who presides and who claims cousinhood
with both the Vaudrys and the Direnni on evidence he prefers not to produce,
decides who reads it. He will not decide in favor of an Argonian. He might be
persuaded, bought, blackmailed, or simply burgled — and the Wool Hall's upper
room is exactly the sort of place a man who has just learned the Muffle would be
tempted to test it, which is how apprentices get stickings and criminal records in
the same night.

None of these doors gives anything away. Each costs standing, secrecy, service, or
skin, and the GM should make the PC pay before any figure is added to his name.

## 4. The green murmur

There is a thing the PC has that has no name in Glenumbra and that he has never
tried to name himself.

It is not a spell and it cannot be cast. It has no figure, no closing, no draw. It does
not answer when he wants it and it does not announce itself when it comes. What it
does is this: sometimes a room goes very quiet inside him just before it goes loud
outside him. Sometimes his hand is already where it needs to be. Sometimes he
dreams of trees — enormous, pale-barked, breathing trees standing in warm black
water — and the dream has a smell, and the smell is not one he can place, and he
does not mention it to anyone, and he has never mentioned it to anyone, and he
could not say why not.

Argonians call this private undercurrent *the green murmur*, and those few who
speak of it at all say it is the Hist, and say nothing further. There are no Argonians
in Larenne. There is no one within two hundred miles who could tell him whether
what he has is ordinary.

He also has a mark on his hide that he has always had and has always had an
explanation for — an explanation given to him young, by someone who meant well,
that he has never had cause to doubt.

**GM discipline.** The green murmur is not a mechanic and must never become one.
The player cannot invoke it, roll for it, or spend anything on it. It surfaces at the
GM's deliberate choosing, rarely, and always deniably — a stillness someone else
notices and remarks on, a reflex that lands a half-second before thought, a dream
noted in a single unhurried sentence and then dropped. Do not explain it in
narration. Do not have an NPC name it. Do not connect it to any organization, to
the mark, or to the training arc above. If it seems to make the Veiling Arts come
strangely easily to him, that is a thing the reader may notice; it is not a thing the
text says.

## 5. Clan Montalion, at a distance

Vampirism in the Iliac Bay is not a disease so much as a lineage. Eight bloodlines
share this coast, each with its own habits, its own inherited gifts, and its own long
grudges with the other seven, and all eight prefer a quiet arrangement to open
predation — a stable territory, a compliant village, a century of nobody counting
carefully. Clan Montalion holds the Glenumbra countryside by that kind of
arrangement, which is to say that Larenne has never seen one and has stories
anyway: a family on the moor road who went away one autumn and left the shutters
closed, a great-uncle in someone's grandmother's telling who did not age between
two weddings, the standing local advice that nobody sensible walks the moor road
after full dark, given without any reason attached because the reason was forgotten
generations ago and the advice was kept. The old manor out past the dike is
supposedly empty. The chapterhouse does not keep it on the tithe rolls, which is odd,
and Brother Aumery has noticed this, and has done nothing about it.

Two practical notes. Montalion have no appetite for Argonian blood — cold, wrong,
by their reckoning barely worth the trouble — which means the PC could walk that
road for years and be ignored, and which is precisely why the thread can sit
untouched indefinitely. And this stays rumor unless the player steers into it: a name
overheard, a coach that never arrived, an unaccountable gap in a ledger. Never a
confrontation, never an offer, until the player reaches for it.
