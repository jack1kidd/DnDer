# Technology — Dwemer Artifice

*Being a brief and necessarily incomplete account of the Dwarven works, as compiled for the Imperial Geographical Society, 3E 430.*

## The Deep Folk and Their Absence

It is held — and the Imperial Commission on Dwemer Studies has spent four decades failing to hold otherwise — that the Dwemer were not dwarves at all, but a heresy of Mer: elves who studied the world the way a surgeon studies a body, and who, at some hour of the year 1E 700, in the deeps beneath Red Mountain, stopped being anywhere. Not slain. Not scattered. *Absent*, all of them, in every hall from Vvardenfell to the Alik'r, in a single indivisible instant — their tools still warm, their soup still on the boil, their lamps still lit. Every scholar of the Third Empire has a theory. The Telvanni say they unmade themselves on purpose. The Redoran say Azura took them for an old insult. The Commission's own official position, printed at Council expense, is four hundred pages long and concludes that further study is warranted. **No one knows. Nothing found in any ruin has ever said.** Treat any NPC who claims certainty as a liar, a madman, or a cultist — sometimes all three.

What they left behind is the point. Dwemer artifice does not answer to the Aedra or the Daedra. It is not enchanted; the Arcane University has confirmed this to its considerable irritation, since a Mysticism adept can read the weave of a Nord grave-hex from across a room and reads *nothing at all* off a Dwarven lamp that has been burning without fuel since before the First Empire. The machinery is cold, mathematical, and indifferent. It ran before the Empire and it will run after. That indifference is the horror of the ruins: not malice, but the total absence of any interest in you.

## The Ruins

There are no Dwemer ruins in Cyrodiil. The Heartlands' bones are Ayleid — white stone and welkynd glass — and a Colovian farmer who tells you he ploughed up "a dwarf hall" has ploughed up an elf cellar. Dwarven halls lie elsewhere: thick under Morrowind's Velothi range and Vvardenfell's ash (**Arkngthand**, **Nchuleftingth**, **Bthungthumz**), through Skyrim's under-mountain (**Mzinchaleft**, **Alftand**, and the half-drowned **Mzeneldt** on the Skyrim shoulder of the Bruma pass), and sunk in Hammerfell's sand at **Volenfell**. Cyrodiil gets the *cargo*, not the ruin — which is precisely why the Imperial City is the best market in Tamriel for Dwarven scrap and the worst place in Tamriel to be caught carrying it unlicensed.

A ruin is not a dungeon that has gone quiet. It is a machine that has been running unsupervised for seventeen centuries: pistons still stroking behind the walls, steam still venting on a schedule nobody set, a great brass sun-clock in a flooded gallery still turning to mark hours in a calendar the world no longer keeps. Doors open when the mechanism decides they open. Bridges extend for reasons. Somewhere below, water is being pumped somewhere, for something.

## Animunculi

The ruins keep their own staff. **Spiders** — knee-high, brass, quick, and swarming — are maintenance: they come for the intrusion the way a house comes for a rat, and they are the ones that kill careless first-timers. **Spheres** travel folded into a ball down the corridors and unfold mid-roll into a bladed thing with one arm and one crossbow, which is exactly as unpleasant to meet in a stairwell as it sounds. **Centurions**, ten feet of steam and hammer-armed bronze, stand in their alcoves venting quietly for centuries and step down when a threshold is crossed — and a party that has cheerfully carved through forty spiders should be told, plainly and in advance, that a centurion is a different order of problem. **Steam centurions** are worse and rarer. There is talk, mostly Telvanni talk, of things further down that are neither.

None of them are alive. None of them are undead. Conjuration does not bind them, Illusion does not fool them, turning does nothing; they have no mind to frighten and no soul to leverage. They are also not *guards* in any sense a mortal would recognize — they do not pursue past a boundary, do not coordinate, do not care who you are or what you took, and will ignore a thief standing perfectly still in a doorway while shredding his friend three feet away. Their logic is real, consistent, and unpublished. Players who watch carefully can learn a ruin's rules. Players who assume the rules are the same as the last ruin's die.

## Loot, Hazard, Wonder — Never a Tech Tree

Nobody in 3E 430 builds this. Nobody repairs it. The most a mortal smith achieves is *reforging*: melting Dwarven metal — that dull warm gold-brown alloy nobody has reproduced — into armor and blades that are lighter and better than steel and utterly mundane in every other way. A handful of Dunmer scrapsmiths and one or two Orsimer in the Empire can work it at all; everyone else spoils the metal and sells the ruin of it as curiosity.

So Dwemer things enter play as three faces of the same object:
- **Loot.** Cogs, gears, struts, and dynamo cores fetch real septims from Commission agents, Mages Guild researchers, and collectors who do not ask about permits. A centurion's dynamo core is a small fortune and heavy enough to make you choose between it and the exit.
- **Hazard.** Steam vents, ceiling-mounted blade-arms, a lift that answers a lever after a six-second pause, and animunculi that wake on a trigger the party will never identify. The trap is not a trap; it is a machine doing its job with you in the way.
- **Wonder.** A gallery of brass reeds the height of a cathedral that hums when you shout in it. A wall of etched Dwemeris that the finest translator in the Empire can render as *"— and therefore the twelfth is inadmissible —"* and no more. An unbroken lamp. These beats should land as awe, not as a puzzle to solve.

Give the players Dwemer artifice as an *encounter with something older and better than them that does not acknowledge them*. Let them take metal from it. Never let them master it, and never — under any circumstance, however clever the question — let the GM explain where the Dwemer went.

## The Law of It

The Elder Council's **Antiquities Statute** makes every Dwemer site Imperial property and every unlicensed excavation a crime, prosecuted by the **Imperial Commission on Dwemer Studies** — a body with more clerks than diggers and an inspector's writ that outranks most provincial magistrates. In Morrowind this collides with Great House law: Hlaalu sells permits it doesn't hold, Redoran considers the whole enterprise grave-robbing regardless of who signed what, and Telvanni excavate whatever they please and dare the Commission to come say so. The paperwork is a genuine adventure obstacle. So is the fence who can make it unnecessary.
