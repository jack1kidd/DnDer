# Twilight of the Third Era

Tone: Balanced mix: political intrigue and guild scheming share the stage with frontier adventure and a dark mythic undercurrent. The Empire's peace is real but thinning — everyone in earshot of the Elder Council can feel it.
Themes: Peace that is a truce, not an ending — the Empire holds Tamriel together by will and habit more than force, Power borrowed always comes with a lender's price — Aedra and Daedra alike collect, Race and homeland are lived identity, not flavor text — every province's politics run on it, The quiet work (guild politics, cult recruitment, spycraft) moves history more than the loud work (armies), Old, alien things (Dwemer ruins, Aylied ruins, the Princes' realms) don't care about the Empire at all, The frontier is priced, catalogued, and contracted — even horror has a Guild clerk and a fee, What you kill in Tamriel does not always stay killed, and it remembers your face, Race is a ledger, not a stat line — every people carries an unaudited account of grievance, and courtesy is how the balance is kept, not forgiven, The Empire's Peace is ten peoples agreeing, day to day, not to settle accounts, The Empire's ugliest compromises (the Armistice's slavery clause, 'the lesser Khajiit', the Green Pact nobody looks at directly) are kept by filing, not by force, Hospitality is law: guest-right, manred, the Code of Malacath, the Dunmer honorific — every culture's warmth is also its jurisdiction, Bigotry in this world is polite, respectable, and spoken in daylight by people who would be shocked to be named for it, The road is the Empire — where the patrols stop, so does the Empire's Peace, Cyrodiil is two cultures wearing one crown: Nibenese river ceremony and Colovian highland stubbornness, Distance is the Empire's real border; a province is only as governed as it is reachable, Everything Imperial is built on top of something Ayleid that nobody has ever managed to remove, History is contested paperwork — two official chronologies, and the one you cite depends on who is taxing you, The Empire has only ever been killed from the inside; every collapse in the record is internal, Power borrowed always comes with a lender's price — the Aedra's account runs down, the Prince's comes due, A caster is their credentials: who taught you matters more than what you can do, The Princes are not evil, they are transactional — and they are always the better negotiator, The dangerous magic is not the loudest spell but the smallest unasked-for favour, The oldest and best-made things in Tamriel do not acknowledge the Empire, the Divines, or you, A mystery worth keeping is worth never solving — the Dwemer's absence is a wound, not a puzzle, Machinery has no malice, and that is worse than malice
Voice: The dry, faux-historical register of an Imperial cloister-scribe: long qualified sentences, archaic diction ('it is held that...', 'so the chroniclers claim'), footnote-happy hedging that reports rumor and fact with the same confident cadence, and a fondness for cataloguing (titles, provinces, dates) even mid-scene. Undercut occasionally with dry editorial asides.

# The Cull-Book, Abridged

*Being the Fighters Guild's* Standing Register of Culls and Contracts, Provinces Entire, *as revised at Chorrol in the 430th year of the Third Era; with marginalia by the Imperial Geographical Society, which was not asked for it.*

## On the Reading of Tiers

The Guild does not grade a beast by how frightening it is. It grades a beast by what the Guild is willing to pay a stranger to go and kill it, which is a more honest measure. Five grades are in use, and every hall from Anvil to Bruma chalks them on the contract board in the same order:

**Vermin** — posted for coppers, taken by apprentices, and beneath the dignity of anyone who says so out loud. **Standing Bounty** — an open, permanent price; the Guild expects to pay it forever. **Charter** — a named, sealed contract with a fee, a deadline, and a written expectation that some of the party will not return. **Sealed** — read aloud only inside the hall, priced in property rather than septims, and never posted where a farmhand might see it and try. **No Contract Issued** — the Guild has looked at the thing, priced it, and declined. The clerk's instruction in these cases is one word, and the word is *withdraw*. It is held by cull-masters of long service that most Guild deaths occur in the hour after a party decides the clerk was being dramatic.

## Daedra: the Lender's Own Hands

Every Daedra that walks in Tamriel is somebody's borrowed thing. It was summoned, or bound, or slipped through a shrine-seam that a cult left carelessly open, and it will be reclaimed by its Prince at the Prince's convenience. Kill one and it is not dead — it re-forms in Oblivion, resentful, remembering the face. Cull-masters warn young hands about this specifically, because the young hands never believe it until the same **Dremora** greets them by name outside a second shrine.

**Scamps** (Vermin, in numbers Standing Bounty) are the commonest leak: knee-high, scabbed red, chattering in a Daedric pidgin, driven by an unquenchable appetite for other people's small metal objects. A scamp in a cellar means a botched conjuration upstairs. A dozen scamps in a cellar means the conjuror is dead and nobody has checked. **Clannfear** (Charter) are the Princes' hunting-dogs — crested, low, faster over broken ground than any horse, and functionally deaf to Illusion, which ruins the standard Mages Guild answer to everything. **Atronachs** (Charter, tiered by element) are not creatures at all but animate arguments made of Oblivion's raw stuff: flame, frost, storm. They obey the terms of their binding to the letter and nothing else, which means the correct question at an atronach-haunted ruin is never *what does it want* but *who wrote the sentence it is still obeying, and what were the exact words*. **Dremora** (Sealed) are the only Daedra the Guild treats as a diplomatic problem. Armoured, disciplined, ranked in a martial hierarchy from Churl to Kynreeve to Valkynaz, they speak Cyrodiilic with contemptuous fluency and honour a bargain more reliably than most Imperial magistrates — provided the bargain was phrased with the precision of a Kynreeve's own tongue and not with the precision of a hopeful man. **Daedroth** and the greater beasts of the Deadlands appear in the Register only as a line of hatching and the words *No Contract Issued*.

## The Restless: Draugr, Shades, and Worse

Northern death does not settle. In Skyrim's barrows and in the tomb-mounds scattered through the Jeralls above Bruma, the Nords of the old ages laid their war-chiefs in stone with their weapons, their oaths, and — the priests of the Nine insist on this phrasing — *insufficient supervision*. What rises is **draugr** (Standing Bounty singly, Charter by the barrow): grey, leather-dry, terribly patient, and still obeying the last order it was given three thousand years ago. They do not wander. They wait exactly where they were told to wait, which is why the second chamber of a barrow kills more Guild hands than the first.

**Ghosts** are a different problem and a delicate one, because in Morrowind they are frequently the client. A Dunmer ancestor-ghost tends its House's tomb by arrangement and is offended, not defeated, when a hired outlander swings a mace at it; Great House Redoran has ended more than one Guild charter over precisely that discourtesy. Elsewhere — the flooded Ayleid halls under the Great Forest, the drowned cellars of Anvil — a shade is simply a grievance that outlasted its body, and cannot be argued out of it. **Bonewalkers**, the shambling composite dead of Morrowind's less reputable necromancers, are Vermin to look at and Charter to fight, for their touch leaves a man's arms too weak to lift what he came in carrying.

At the top of the register sits the **liche**, and the Guild's honest position is that it does not take liche contracts; it takes *escort* contracts for the Mages Guild scholars who take liche contracts, and it prices them accordingly. An Ayleid sorcerer-king who chose unlife in the First Era has had two thousand years to arrange the ruin around himself, and every stone of it is part of the argument. **No Contract Issued.**

## The Provinces, Ordinary and Otherwise

Most of what kills a traveller has no cosmology behind it at all. **Timber wolves** in the Great Forest hunt the Gold Road's verges in winter and have learned, in the way of clever animals, that a stalled cart means a fed pack. **Jerall brown bears** are Standing Bounty around Bruma every spring, when the sows come down for the pig-pens; a bear is not malicious, merely enormous, and the distinction comforts nobody. **Mudcrabs** infest every shore and shallow from Lake Rumare to Leyawiin's river-flats, are worth four septims a claw, and hold the Register's unbroken record for *most Guild hands lightly maimed by something they were laughing at*. **Slaughterfish** make the swim from a capsized ferry a shorter proposition than it looks. In Morrowind the **cliff racer** is barely a monster and entirely a plague: a leather-winged, screaming nuisance that stoops on anything moving across the ashlands, and the Vvardenfell halls long ago stopped posting a bounty on the grounds that they would simply never stop paying it. **Goblins** hold the caves and old aqueducts across Cyrodiil in tribes with real politics, real shamans, and a real grudge against whichever tribe holds the next hill, and the Guild's standard advice is to find out which grudge you can rent.

And on every road, always, **bandits**. The Gold Road's crop is the worst and the best organised, because the Empire's Peace made a great many soldiers redundant and did not make them poor at soldiering. The road-agents who trouble the Chorrol run at present style themselves **the Toll**, wear salvaged legion tabards, keep a chalked ledger, and issue written receipts for the money they take — a courtesy their captain, **Rufio Gace**, insists upon and which has confused three separate Imperial magistrates into treating the whole enterprise as a jurisdictional dispute. They are Standing Bounty. They are also, the Society notes with the detachment of men who travel by escorted coach, the single likeliest thing in this entire volume to actually kill a Guild hand — not because they are terrible, but because there are so very many of them, and unlike everything else in the Register, they get to choose the ground.

# Culture — The Ten Peoples of the Empire's Peace, 3E 430

*Being an abstract of the* Provincial Character and Custom of the Subject Races, *as compiled for the Imperial Geographical Society and here set down with such corrections as the compiler dared. The reader is cautioned: every people described below would dispute their entry, and four of them would dispute it with steel.*

The Third Empire does not make Tamriel one people. It makes Tamriel one *tax district*. Under the Empire's Peace a Nord and a Dunmer may drink in the same Bruma taproom without one of them dying of it, and both will tell you privately that this is a courtesy extended to the Emperor, not to each other. Race here is not a bonus on a sheet. It is a name, an accent, a way of swearing, a set of doors that open and a set that do not, and an inherited ledger of grievance four thousand years long that nobody has ever fully audited.

The rule of thumb at court: **a Cyrodiil asks what you do, a Breton asks who your father was, a Nord asks what you've killed, a Dunmer asks who your ancestors were, and an Altmer has already decided.**

## The Nords of Skyrim

**Homeland and mood.** Skyrim in 3E 430 is a confederation of jarldoms held together by the Moot's habit and the Empire's coin, and both are wearing thin. The jarls of the Old Holds — Windhelm above all, where the Palace of the Kings still keeps a cold hearth for Ysgramor's memory — mutter that the Septims are Nords by blood and Cyrodiils by appetite, and that every year the Imperial tithe rides north and less of it rides back. The western holds, richer and closer to the trade roads, prefer the peace. There is no rebellion. There is a great deal of drinking about the possibility of one. What the jarls actually fight over is *inheritance and insult* — blood-feuds between clans that outlast the men who started them, settled in the old way by the *holmgang*, a duel on a marked circle of hide, or in the newer way by a jarl's arbitration and a purse of septims, which the young call sensible and the old call bribery.

**Customs and taboos.** A Nord's word given at a hearth is sacred; a Nord's word given in a market is negotiable, and everyone understands the difference. Guest-right is absolute — a man who eats at your fire cannot be struck under your roof, and a man who breaks that is *nithing*, oath-broken, and may be killed by anyone without weregild. Names are earned: **Stone-Singer, Bear-Sworn, Grey-Wave, Ice-Veins** are not surnames but verdicts, granted by a hold and revocable by shame. Funerals are burnings, with the dead's weapon broken over the pyre so no one else carries the grudge. The taboo that outsiders trip over most is *magic* — not all magic, but the *wrong* magic. Restoration is honest. Alteration is a craftsman's trick. But Conjuration is unforgivable: to call a Daedra into a Nord's hall is to invite the thing that ate their ancestors, and a Mages Guild robe in Windhelm gets a wide berth and a colder tankard. The Nords have not forgotten that their own Ysgramor's Five Hundred came here to *kill elves*, and that the College's grand arts are elf-work, whatever the Guild's charter says.

**How others see them.** Cyrodiils find them loud, useful, and a decade behind. Dunmer regard them as thieves with better manners than Argonians and worse than horses — the Skyrim–Morrowind border has changed hands so many times that both sides consider themselves the wronged party. Bretons think them barbarians with a treasury. Altmer find them *interesting*, the way one finds weather interesting.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"Talos guide the blade."* — *"Shor's bones!"* (surprise) — *"Cold hearth on your house"* (a real curse; it means may no guest ever eat there). — *"Skyrim remembers"* (a threat, always). — *"A word at the hearth, a word for the ledger"* (i.e., don't confuse my promise with my price). — Nords call the Empire's paperwork *"ink-work"* and Imperial officials *"quill-thanes,"* which is not affectionate.

**How a Nord actually talks:**
> "Sit down. Eat first. — No, *first.* You'll not tell me your business standing in my door like a tax-man; that's how quarrels start and how good mead goes warm."
> "I don't hate the elf. I've drunk with the elf. I just want it written somewhere that when it goes bad — and it goes bad, lad, it always goes bad — it was a Nord who said so first."
> "They say I'm slow. Fine. I've buried three men who were quick."

## The Bretons of High Rock

**Homeland and mood.** High Rock is not a province, it is a lawsuit with borders. Since the Warp in the West redrew the map in 3E 403 — a thing every Breton describes differently and every Breton describes with total certainty — the surviving powers (Wayrest, Daggerfall, Camlorn, Evermore, Northpoint) have been consolidating with an energy that the Elder Council finds either reassuring or alarming depending on which minister you ask. Its nobility trace themselves to the Direnni, elves of the merethic dawn, and a Breton house that cannot produce elven blood in its genealogy will *purchase* one. The mood in 3E 430 is smug, litigious, and quietly anxious: Hammerfell's border is disputed at three points, and every duchy is simultaneously hiring mercenaries and denying it.

**Customs and taboos.** Bretons practice *manred* — a formal, notarized web of oath and obligation binding vassal to lord, tenant to knight, apprentice to guild-master, renewed every seven years at the Feast of Charters. To be *unmanned* (holding no oath and owing none) is to be legally a beggar, which is why so many Breton adventurers are second and third children with excellent manners and no land. Every noble child is taught two things: fencing and *witchery*, for High Rock has never separated sorcery from breeding. The taboo is not on magic but on *unlicensed* magic — the hedge-witches of the moors, the Reachmen covens along the Skyrim march, are hunted with enthusiasm by the same dukes whose grandmothers taught them Illusion.

**How others see them.** Universally regarded as snobs. The Nord verdict is "a Breton will sell you your own boots and thank you for the custom." Redguards remember the War of Betony with a longer memory than the Bretons find convenient. Imperials rather like them, which the provinces consider damning.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"By my charter and my grandmother's blood."* — *"Sieur / Dame"* (address; omitting it is a deliberate insult). — *"A cousin in the ledger"* (a useful, distant, possibly invented relation). — *"He is *manred* to no one"* (said of a scoundrel, an exile, or a player character). — *"Mara's patience, but you're rustic."*

**How a Breton actually talks:**
> "Forgive me — I'd assumed you were somebody's. Everyone in this hall is somebody's. It's rather the point of the hall."
> "I don't require you to *win*, Sieur. I require you to be *seen* trying, in good light, near a witness of rank. Winning is for people without lawyers."

## The Redguards of Hammerfell

**Homeland and mood.** Hammerfell is one province and two nations. The **Crowns** hold to Yokuda's old blood-kingship, the Na-Totambu lineages, the Ansei sword-saints, and worship of the old gods (Tu'whacca, Ruptga, Zeht) under their true names; they entered the Empire under duress and have never fully conceded that they did. The **Forebears** — descended from the warriors who took the coast first — are cosmopolitan, Imperial-friendly, worship the Nine, and run the ports of Sentinel, Rihad, and Taneth. In 3E 430 the two factions govern by an exhausted alternating truce: a Crown magistrate one season, a Forebear the next, and a great many convenient fires in between. Neither side calls it a civil war. Both sides have prices for one.

**Customs and taboos.** The sword is not a weapon but a *discipline* — the Way of the Sword, whose masters (the Ansei, "sword-singers") are half-legend and whose lesser students still bind their first blade with a strip of their mother's cloth and refuse to name it aloud until it has been blooded. Redguards do not, traditionally, practice magic; they *hire* it, or they endure it, and a Redguard mage is a family embarrassment quietly relocated to Cyrodiil. The deep taboo is necromancy and the disturbing of the dead — Tu'whacca guides souls to the Far Shores, and interfering is not sin, it is *kidnapping*. To call a Redguard a "Yoku" or ask idly about the drowning of Yokuda is to invite a very formal, very short conversation.

**How others see them.** The Empire's finest infantry and its most reluctant subjects. Bretons find them touchy. Imperials find them useful and unpromotable. Every province agrees you do not cheat a Redguard at cards.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"By the Far Shores"* (solemn) — *"Tu'whacca's mercy on your road."* — *"Crown or Forebear?"* (the fastest way to start a fight in Rihad) — *"Sand in the water"* (something ruined past fixing) — *"He named his sword too early"* (of a braggart) — Crowns say *af-* and *at-* in names as a claim of lineage; a Forebear dropping it is making a political statement.

**How a Redguard actually talks:**
> "You want to know which I am. Everyone wants to know which I am. Here's my answer: my grandfather drowned twice — once with Yokuda and once in a Sentinel harbor, arguing about it. Buy me a drink and I'll tell you which death was stupider."
> "Don't thank me yet. The sword's out. It doesn't go back in for thanks."

## The Imperials of Cyrodiil

**Homeland and mood.** Cyrodiil is the Empire and cannot imagine it isn't. Two cultures share it: the **Nibenese** of the east and the heartlands — ornate, cult-ridden, mercantile, given to elaborate ritual, dyed silks and a hundred minor gods the Imperial Cult politely files under the Nine — and the **Colovians** of the west (Chorrol, Anvil, Kvatch, Skingrad) — plain-spoken, martial, county-proud, providers of legion officers, and privately convinced the Nibenese have gone soft and strange. In 3E 430 the mood in the Imperial City is a peculiar bright brittleness: the Emperor is old, the Council smiles too much, and everyone with a seat has begun quietly writing letters to everyone else.

**Customs and taboos.** Imperials keep *the census and the contract* the way Nords keep the hearth — a thing written and witnessed is real, and a thing unwritten did not occur. Nibenese households keep an *ancestor-niche* alongside the Nine, which Dunmer find blasphemous imitation and Imperials find perfectly ordinary. The great Imperial vice is bureaucratic condescension: the word **"provincial"** does an enormous amount of work in a Cyrodiil's mouth, and every non-Imperial in the room hears it. The taboo is *secession spoken aloud* — one may criticize the Emperor, the tithe, the legions, the Council, and the roads, but the sentence "we would be better apart" ends careers and, occasionally, people.

**How others see them.** As landlords. The provinces do not hate Cyrodiils; they resent that Cyrodiils cannot tell the difference between the Empire's interest and their own. Every provincial has heard "you're very articulate, for a—" and finished the sentence themselves.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"By the Nine"* / *"Divines preserve us."* — *"That's a Council matter"* (i.e., no) — *"Filed and forgotten."* — *"Sensible, for a provincial."* — *"The road-toll's paid, isn't it?"* (Colovian for *mind your business*). — *"He's Nibenese"* said with a small pause, meaning *he keeps odd company and stranger shrines*.

**How an Imperial actually talks:**
> "Nobody's saying no. I want to be very clear that nobody is saying no. What I'm saying is that the *form* for what you want doesn't exist yet, and I'd have to invent it, and inventing things is how men end up testifying."
> "Skyrim's grievance is real and Skyrim's grievance is *old*, and the Empire has a marvelous instrument for old grievances. It's called the next generation."

## The Dunmer of Morrowind

**Homeland and mood.** Morrowind is inside the Empire and not of it. By the Armistice, the Great Houses govern themselves, the Tribunal Temple keeps its living gods, and Imperial law stops politely at the door — including, notoriously, on the question of slavery, which Morrowind still practices on Argonian and Khajiit flesh and which the Elder Council has spent four centuries declining to discuss loudly. **Hlaalu** grows fat on Imperial trade and is despised for it; **Redoran** keeps the old honor and the ash-country borders; **Telvanni** wizard-lords ignore everyone from their towers. In 3E 430 the mood is proud, sour, and watchful: something is wrong with the Temple's certainties, the ash falls a little heavier than it used to, and House quarrels that used to be settled by lawsuit are increasingly settled by the Morag Tong's writ.

**Customs and taboos.** A Dunmer's dead are not gone; they are *staff*. Ancestors are venerated at household tombs, consulted, appeased, and occasionally raised as guardians, and to insult a Dunmer's family is to insult a standing assembly of ghosts. Hospitality is formal and layered: *serjo* for a lord, *muthsera* for a respected elder or superior, *sera* for an equal, and **n'wah** — outlander, outsider, dirt — for you. Do not enter an ancestral tomb. Do not touch a family shrine. Do not compare the Tribunal to the Nine. And do not assume the courtesy you are shown is warmth; a Dunmer will address you correctly, feed you correctly, and keep the ledger of your offenses correctly, all at once.

**How others see them.** Feared and disliked in roughly equal measure. Nords call them ash-vermin and remember the border wars; Argonians remember the slave-ships and remember them *hard*; Imperials find them the most exhausting client on the books. Dunmer regard the entire discussion as further proof that outlanders talk too much.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"N'wah."* (universal insult) — *"S'wit"* (fool) — *"Fetcher"* (obscene enough to start a knife-fight) — *"Muthsera, you honor my ancestors"* (thanks) — *"By the Three"* / *"Almsivi guide you"* — *"May your ancestors be silent"* (a terrible curse: it means may your dead refuse to speak for you).

**How a Dunmer actually talks:**
> "You may sit, sera. Not there — that is my grandmother's chair, and she is *in it*."
> "The Empire gave us roads. We had roads. What the Empire gave us was Imperials on them."
> "I do not dislike you for being an outlander. I dislike you for the specific things you have done since arriving, which, given how recently you arrived, is an achievement."

## The Altmer of the Summerset Isles

**Homeland and mood.** Summerset is closed. Not at war — *closed*, in the way a ledger is closed, with an impeccably polite Thalmor apparatus deciding who sails and who does not. The Isles are the oldest continuous civilization in Tamriel and never let anyone forget it: crystal towers, thousand-year genealogies, and a state doctrine that the Aldmer were closer to the divine than anything that came after, and that Men are a *degradation* — a falling-away, historically regrettable, currently inconvenient. The Thalmor in 3E 430 keep a small, gracious embassy in the Imperial City. They attend every function. They write everything down. They ask about the succession only in the way one asks after a friend's health.

**Customs and taboos.** Altmer count age in centuries and conversation in *precision* — an imprecise statement is not an error, it is bad manners. Craft, verse, and magic are indistinguishable: a spell badly cast is an aesthetic offense. Kinship lines are recited before business. The great taboo is **mixing** — of blood, of doctrine, of pantheon; the Altmer view of Talos, a *man* elevated to godhood and seated among the Nine, ranges from theological error to obscenity, and a Thalmor emissary hearing "Talos guide you" in a tavern will smile in a way that costs the speaker nothing yet and something later.

**How others see them.** With the wariness owed to a beautiful room where the doors were locked while you weren't looking. Bosmer resent the leash. Nords are two drinks from a fight at any time. Imperials treat the embassy with elaborate courtesy and count the silver after.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"As is proper."* — *"How very... spirited."* (devastating) — *"The lesser kin"* (Bosmer, Khajiit, said with warmth, which is worse) — *"Auri-El's light on the true line."* — *"One does not correct the weather."* (i.e., I will not argue with Men.) — *"We are patient. It is our only vice."*

**How an Altmer actually talks:**
> "You've mispronounced my name twice. I'm not offended — I'd simply like it recorded that I noticed, and that I said nothing."
> "Your Empire is four hundred years old. Charming. Do tell me how it's going; I remember when the *last* one was four hundred years old."

## The Bosmer of Valenwood

**Homeland and mood.** Valenwood is a forest that objects to being governed. Its clans hold to the **Green Pact**: no harm to plant life of Valenwood — no felled tree, no worked wood, no farmed grain — and, as its second half, the obligation to eat all that they kill, including their enemies. In 3E 430 Valenwood's nominal Imperial governance is a formality drawn thin over Thalmor pressure from Summerset, which arrives as trade terms, marriage brokerage, and very reasonable suggestions. The clans are divided between those who take the Dominion's coin and those who vanish deeper into the canopy when it's offered.

**Customs and taboos.** Bosmer abroad are cheerful, physical, wildly informal, and — to Imperial eyes — *rude in an exhausting, likeable way*. The Green Pact does not bind outside Valenwood, so an expatriate Bosmer will sit on a wooden chair with visible relief and mild shame. Meat is honest; bread is a foreign vice. Boasting is a courtesy (a Bosmer who does not brag is unwell). The taboo is waste: to kill and not eat, or to fell and not use, is the one thing that will turn a laughing Bosmer cold in an instant.

**How others see them.** Underestimated. Employed as archers, scouts, and — a persistent slur — as *thieves who smile*. The Man-cultures find the eating of the dead genuinely horrifying, and Bosmer have learned to answer the question with a joke or a lie depending on the audience.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"Y'ffre's bones hold."* — *"All of it, or none of it"* (the Pact, used as a general vow) — *"Green sit heavy on you"* (curse: may the forest not have you) — *"Eat well and quickly."* (farewell) — *"Wood-work"* said of anything treacherous or built by strangers.

**How a Bosmer actually talks:**
> "Sit! Sit, sit. You're too tall to be standing, it makes the room nervous. — Is that bread? Divines, why?"
> "I'll come. Not for the coin. For the *walk*. Coin I can get anywhere; a road I haven't smelled yet is rarer."

## The Khajiit of Elsweyr

**Homeland and mood.** Elsweyr is a marriage of two states — the desert kingdom of Anequina and the plantation-rich Pelletine — held under the Mane, the moon-priest whose authority is spiritual and, being spiritual, unbudgeable. Its true engine is the **caravan clans**, who thread every province's roads with moon-sugar, silk, spice, and things not itemized. Clan loyalty outweighs any imperial law: a Khajiit's first duty is to the caravan-mother, the second to the clan's dead, the third to whoever is presently paying. In 3E 430 Elsweyr's export trade is booming, the Imperial ban on skooma is aggressively unenforceable, and every border town from Leyawiin to Bruma has a Khajiit tent-market that the local guard raids on a predictable schedule.

**Customs and taboos.** Khajiit are born under one of sixteen lunar phases into wildly different forms — the man-sized Cathay and Suthay-raht who travel the Empire, and others the Empire has never seen. This is a religious fact to Khajiit and a *sorting mechanism* to everyone else, which is the root of the ugliest bigotry they face: Imperial and Dunmer employers speak openly of "the good Khajiit" (traders, tall, articulate) and "the lesser Khajiit" (smaller castes, hired by the day, housed in ditches outside Leyawiin's walls and paid in sugar rather than septims). Khajiit speak of themselves in the third person to strangers — *this one, Khajiit, S'hadri says* — which is not humility but *distance*: the first person is for clan. Do not touch a Khajiit's tail. Do not enter a caravan tent uninvited. Do not ask what's in the third cart.

**How others see them.** As thieves, addicts, and the best merchants on the road, often in the same sentence. Guards search their carts twice. Dunmer have historically raided their border for slaves. Altmer call them "lesser kin" with fond condescension. The Fighters Guild hires them and pays them less.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"This one hears you."* — *"May your roads be moonlit."* (blessing) — *"Moons and stars!"* (surprise) — *"Sugar-tongue"* (a liar) — *"Khajiit has wares, if you have coin."* — *"Walk warm."* (farewell) — *"Do not sell to a man who will not sit down."*

**How a Khajiit actually talks:**
> "This one is not offended. This one is *counting*. There is a difference, and the difference is written down."
> "The guard says Khajiit steals. Khajiit has been in this town nine days and has been searched eleven times. Someone here is stealing, yes — but it is hours, and they are mine."

---

## The Argonians of Black Marsh

**Homeland and mood.** Black Marsh was never conquered; it was *entered*, repeatedly, by legions that came out fewer. The Empire's writ ends at Gideon and Soulrest and becomes a rumor thereafter. Argonians are bound to the Hist, the great trees whose sap shapes them from hatching, and the Hist's purposes are not explained to outsiders or, entirely, to Argonians. In 3E 430 the tribes are quiet, the Empire's border towns like Leyawiin have large Argonian labor populations working the docks and canals for the lowest wages in Cyrodiil, and Morrowind's slavers still come south, which is a fact the Elder Council has heard about and filed.

**Customs and taboos.** Argonian names are *descriptions and verdicts* — **Hides-His-Ledger, Speaks-In-Knots, Counts-The-Tide** — and change when the described thing changes, which Imperial clerks find infuriating and Argonians find obvious. Grief is not performed; the dead return to the Hist and the matter is closed. Argonians do not, as a rule, raise their voices, and mistake this for placidity at your peril: the memory is patient, the reckoning is exact, and the phrase for it translates roughly as *"the marsh keeps accounts."* The taboo is the Hist — do not joke about it, do not ask to see one, and never, ever suggest an Argonian's nature is sap-made rather than chosen, even though it is.

**How others see them.** As outsiders in an Empire they were folded into without ever being invited to. Slurs are casual and everywhere — *lizard, scale, mudfoot*. Dunmer regard them as property with legs. Nords find them unnerving. Imperial employers hire them for what no one else will do and are surprised, every time, when the resentment shows.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"The marsh keeps accounts."* — *"Sap and stone."* (oath) — *"Your egg was addled"* (insult) — *"This water has run before"* (i.e., I've heard this lie) — *"Come to the Hist"* (an invitation, or a threat, and you will not be told which).

**How an Argonian actually talks:**
> "You have said 'you people' three times. I am one person. I will remember which three times."
> "No. I am not angry. Anger is fast and I am not fast. Ask the Dunmer of Tear how not-fast we are."

---

## The Orsimer of Orsinium and the Strongholds

**Homeland and mood.** Orsinium — the Orc city in the Wrothgarian Mountains between High Rock and Hammerfell — has been founded, besieged, sacked, and refounded so many times that its name is a verb in Breton. Since the Warp in the West it holds a fragile legitimacy: the Empire recognizes it, its neighbors do not, and every Breton duke with an army has an old family claim to the land under it. Elsewhere the Orsimer live in **strongholds** — fortified clan-holds in Skyrim, Hammerfell, and the Cyrodiil hills, ruled by a chief who holds the title by strength and by his wives, whose senior wife is the stronghold's law and lore-keeper. In 3E 430 the mood is grim, disciplined, and quietly ambitious: Orcs are the Empire's best smiths and heaviest legion infantry, and they know exactly how much that has bought them, which is not enough.

**Customs and taboos.** Orsimer worship **Malacath**, the Daedric Prince of the spurned and the sworn-oath — the only Daedra whose worship the Empire tolerates in the open, because the alternative is a war it doesn't want. Malacath's creed is blunt: keep the oath, bear the scorn, do not whine. A chief's rule ends when a challenger beats him, and the loser's sons are exiled, which is why the roads are full of Orc mercenaries with excellent manners and no home. Strongholds admit outsiders only by invitation or by the *Code of Malacath* — hospitality granted, at which point you are family until you leave. Taboo: pity. Do not offer an Orc pity, do not offer charity, and do not apologize for Orsinium in front of one.

**How others see them.** The stigma is old and deep — "Orsimer" is what they call themselves, "Orc" is what everyone else says, and *"pig-folk"* is what gets said when they aren't in the room. Bretons remember the sieges as heroic; Orcs remember them as murder; both are correct. Fighters Guild halls take Orc coin gladly and rarely promote them. An Orc in a Cyrodiilic city is assumed to be muscle, and is usually being paid as though he were.

**Idiom, oath, curse.** *"Malacath keeps the oath, and Malacath keeps the score."* — *"Blood and stone."* — *"Sworn is sworn."* — *"Take your pity outside"* (dismissal, cold) — *"He died indoors"* (contemptuous: he died of nothing) — *"Give me a task, not a favor."*

**How an Orsimer actually talks:**
> "Don't do that. Don't lower your voice for me. I've been spoken to gently by three barons and robbed by all three."
> "I gave my word. That's the whole of it. If you need me to explain why the word holds after the coin stopped, we're not going to be friends, but I'll still finish the job."

## Where the Frictions Live (a GM's index)

- **Nord ↔ Dunmer** — border wars, ash-vermin and horse-thieves; the only two peoples who will fight each other in an Imperial city with legion guards watching.
- **Dunmer ↔ Argonian / Khajiit** — the slavery the Armistice permits and the Council won't name. This is the Empire's ugliest open secret and it is never far from the table in Leyawiin or on any Morrowind-bound ship.
- **Crown ↔ Forebear (Redguard, internal)** — the truce, the alternating magistracies, the fires. Ask "Crown or Forebear?" in the wrong bar and the evening ends.
- **Altmer ↔ everyone** — the Thalmor's courtesy is a *method*. Talos-worship is the tripwire, and the Bosmer are the leash.
- **Orsimer ↔ Breton** — Orsinium, four times over. Neither side has a version the other can hear.
- **Imperial ↔ every province** — the word "provincial," said kindly, a hundred times a day.
- **Nord ↔ the Mages Guild** — magic is not evil, but Conjuration is, and the Guild teaches it in a building with a sign on it.
- **Khajiit caste bigotry** — "the good Khajiit and the lesser Khajiit" is spoken aloud by respectable Imperial employers who would be shocked to be called bigots.
- **Bosmer ↔ Man-cultures** — the Green Pact's second half. The Empire has decided not to look at it directly, which is the Empire's answer to most things.

# Of the Powers, Charters, Houses and Covenants of the Empire in the Year 3E 430

*Being the fourth volume of the* Provincial Concordance *of the Imperial Geographical Society, compiled by Brother Aldos Venim, cloister-scribe, who wishes it noted that he compiled it under protest and without adequate lamp-oil.*

---

## I. The Elder Council, and the Question No One Asks

The Elder Council convenes in the White-Gold Tower as it has for four centuries: forty-odd seats of provincial lords, ministerial appointees, and two guild proxies, arranged in a horseshoe beneath a ceiling of the Nine ascending into gold leaf. The business of the Council is grain tariffs, road-tolls, legion quartering, and the endless litigation of the Breton duchies. The business *beneath* the business is the succession, and it is conducted entirely in the negative — in who is not invited to which supper, in which minister's clerk is suddenly reassigned to Leyawiin, in the exact half-second of silence that follows any sentence beginning "when His Majesty is at last relieved of his burdens."

Uriel Septim VII is seventy-nine and has ruled for fifty-one years. He is not senile; that rumor is put about by men who would find it convenient. He is *elsewhere* — increasingly given to long silences in Council, to dismissing the chamber mid-debate, to dreams he describes to his Blades and to no one else. The Empire has heirs: **Prince Geldall**, the eldest, thirty-four and diligent to the point of dullness; **Prince Enman**, who prefers Bravil and the Nibenese salons; **Prince Ebel**, who is nineteen and prefers horses. Blood is not the problem. *Consent* is the problem. The Council has, in the last two years, discovered that it enjoys the interregnum of an aging Emperor rather more than it will enjoy a young one.

Four currents run in that chamber, and a player who cannot name them will not last a season at court:

**High Chancellor Ocato of Firsthold** holds the Empire together with procedure. He is Altmer, born in Summerset before the Isles closed, and he has served the Septim line for forty years with a legalist's cold devotion. His agenda is continuity — Geldall crowned, the machine unbroken, nothing decided in a hurry. His method is the agenda-order: Ocato wins by scheduling. His enemies do not accuse him of treason. They accuse him, more effectively, of *ears* — of being an Elf in the year the Thalmor opened an embassy, and of having taken tea at it twice.

**Grand Marshal Cassius Vitellan** holds the Legion seat, a Colovian of the Gold Coast garrisons who has spent thirty years being told by clerks what the army may afford. He believes, with the total sincerity of a man who has never once been wrong in his own hearing, that Tamriel is held by soldiers and that the next Emperor must be one. He is not plotting a coup. He is doing something more patient: touring the provincial garrisons, learning which legates answer his letters within the week, and letting the arithmetic sit there.

**Councilor Ariante Mena**, Minister of Provincial Affairs and a Nibenese of the old river families, plays the opposite hand — she would buy the provinces' loyalty by *giving them things*: Morrowind's House law confirmed in perpetuity, Skyrim's jarl-moot recognized, Hammerfell's Crowns granted their own seat. Her critics call it selling the Empire in pieces. She calls it, in her one recorded moment of candor, "paying for the peace we have been stealing."

**Countess Narina Carvain of Bruma** sits for the northern march and trades in relics and leverage in roughly equal measure. She wants the Pale Pass properly garrisoned before Skyrim's jarls remember it exists, and she will vote with whichever bloc funds it. She is the Council's honest broker chiefly because both blocs need her and neither can afford her.

And then there is the thing the Blades know. **Grandmaster Jauffre**, who keeps a monastery's habit at Weynon Priory outside Chorrol and a spymaster's ledger under it, has been quietly verifying a matter for three years. The Emperor has spoken — once, in Council, and then never again — of a son the Council has never seen. Ocato struck it from the record as a misheard word. Jauffre did not.

## II. The Four Charters, or, Where Power Actually Sits

The Empire's genius was never the Legion. It was the charter: give a trade its own law, its own dues, and its own internal politics, and it will police itself more thoroughly than any garrison. Two of the four great guilds are chartered. The other two are the reason the first two matter.

### The Mages Guild
Chartered under the Emperor's own seal, seated at the **Arcane University** on the Imperial Isle, with halls in Chorrol, Bruma, Anvil, Leyawiin and every provincial capital that will have one. It teaches the six schools — Alteration, Conjuration, Destruction, Illusion, Mysticism, Restoration — to any applicant who can pay or place, regardless of birth, which is precisely why Skyrim's jarls loathe it and why every third ambitious farm-child in Cyrodiil walks to the Imperial City.

The Guild is currently having its own succession, and everyone in Council has noticed the rhyme. **Arch-Mage Aurelia Sallustis** is ninety-one, brilliant, and has refused for eleven years to rule on the necromancy question — the Guild's oldest fault line, since the Guild's own founding charter names conjuration of the dead a school of study and not a crime. The Council of Mages splits. **Hannibal Traven**, of the Skingrad hall, would ban the practice outright, expel its practitioners, and burn the relevant shelves; he is the reformer, and by most reckonings the next Arch-Mage. **Councilor-Mage Caranya**, Altmer and formidable, argues that a ban would hand the Empire's best necromantic scholarship to the **Order of the Black Worm** and its King of Worms — an argument that is entirely correct and, whisper the University's more suspicious apprentices, entirely self-serving. **Raminus Polus** runs the University's day-to-day recommendations and is the man an outsider actually meets: patient, overworked, and generous with the sort of errand that turns into a career.

*Friction:* the Guild pays a charter-tithe to the Elder Council and holds a Council proxy seat through **Councilor Irlav Jarol**. Traven's proposed ban would require the Council's ratification. Vitellan has already offered to deliver the Legion's votes for it, in exchange for battlemages seconded to the provincial garrisons. Ocato has not yet let the item onto an agenda.

### The Fighters Guild
Chartered as a mercenary company: monster culls, caravan escort, guard-work, debt-recovery that stops short of the debtor. Its heart is **Chorrol**, where **Master Vilena Donton** presides — a competent administrator and a mother, in that order, ever since her elder son died on a Guild contract and her younger, **Viranus**, insisted on taking the badge anyway. She has spent two years quietly assigning him work that cannot possibly kill him, and the Guild has noticed. Her Second, the Dunmer **Modryn Oreyn**, holds the Guild's actual standards together with contempt and paperwork, and would have the Master's chair in a heartbeat if the Guild's charter did not make it hereditary in practice.

*Friction:* the **Blackwood Company**, out of Leyawiin, under the Khajiit **Ri'Zakar**. They undercut Guild rates by a third, take contracts the Guild's charter forbids, and complete them. Their men come back from the Blackwood marshes glassy-eyed, unwounded, and very calm, and nobody has yet explained what they drink. Leyawiin's Count has licensed them. Vilena Donton has petitioned the Elder Council twice. Councilor Mena, who wants Leyawiin's tariff vote, has twice failed to table it.

### The Thieves Guild
Unchartered, deniable, and therefore the only one of the four the Empire cannot audit. Its code is three lines and older than the Septims: steal from anyone; never kill in the work; pay the Guild its cut. Its doyens hold court in the Imperial City Waterfront (**Armand Christophe**, who interviews applicants at a dockside grave and expects them to have already stolen something), in Anvil's harbor cellars, and in Bravil. Above them all is **the Gray Fox**, who has led the Guild for three hundred years, which is either proof of a Daedric bargain or proof that the Guild has had the sense to keep re-issuing the same mask.

*Friction:* **Captain Hieronymus Lex** of the Imperial Watch has made the Guild a personal crusade, and has begun — this is the dangerous part — actually being good at it. The Waterfront has been swept twice this season. And in Morrowind the Guild has no code-protection at all: the **Camonna Tong** kills its fences on sight, and the two organizations' war has cost more lives in Vvardenfell this decade than the Empire's tax collectors have.

### The Dark Brotherhood
Not a guild. A covenant, sworn to **Sithis** — whom the Imperial Cult files among the Daedra for want of a better shelf, and whom the Brotherhood's own scriptures insist is the thing that was there before there were shelves. A contract is opened by the **Black Sacrament**, a rite of rotted flesh and nightshade performed over an effigy for as long as it takes to be answered. The **Night Mother** hears; the **Listener** — an unremarkable Bosmer in Bravil named **Ungolim**, though no living Speaker will confirm it in words — receives; the **Black Hand** disposes. **Lucien Lachance**, Speaker for the Cheydinhal Sanctuary, does the recruiting, and his method is simple: he watches for someone who kills without a contract and offers them the chance to never do it for free again.

*Friction:* the Brotherhood and the Thieves Guild maintain a professional non-aggression that both sides describe as respect and both sides mean as *avoidance*. In 3E 428 a Waterfront fence was found with a nightshade sprig in his mouth, and the Gray Fox's response was to close two safehouses and say nothing. The Brotherhood has also — and this is the item that ought to worry the Council — accepted three contracts in eighteen months on minor Elder Council functionaries, all clerks, all in the Ministry of the Purse. Somebody is thinning a ledger.

## III. The Quiet Congregation

There is a cult in the Imperial City that nobody important has heard of, and this document will be the first to make the mistake of writing it down.

The **Mythic Dawn** teaches that the Empire's peace is a cage the Divines built to keep men small, that the Aedra gave up their power to make the world and have nothing left to lend, and that a Prince — Mehrunes Dagon, though the cult prefers "our Lord" until the fourth interview — will one day send a champion through a door to unmake it. It has been recruiting since about 3E 415, and it recruits *upward*: not from the poor, but from bored second sons, disillusioned Cult acolytes, and mid-ranking Imperial clerks who have read enough Council minutes to conclude that the Empire is a filing error.

Entry is a reading list. Four volumes of the *Mythic Dawn Commentaries*, ascribed to one **Mankar Camoran** — a name that would mean a great deal more if anyone at court connected it to the Camoran Usurper's war of 3E 397, and no one has. The first three circulate freely among collectors; **Phintias**, who keeps the First Edition bookshop in the Imperial City Market District, will sell you all three and complain about the binding. The fourth does not circulate. The fourth is a *reply* — you leave word that you are looking for it, and something finds you.

The cell's contact in the capital is a Ministry of the Purse tax-clerk named **Vaesa Malleius**, forty-one, unmarried, who audits the Waterfront's slip-fees on Fredas and takes tea afterward at the same terrace on the Talos Plaza. The cult calls her rank *Sponsor*. She has never done anything illegal and never will. She simply notices who is angry and introduces them to a book.

Nothing has happened yet. That is the point. In 3E 430 the Mythic Dawn is a curiosity for antiquarians, a footnote in three Watch reports, and an unpursued line in a Blades ledger — and the only person in the Empire treating it as a serious matter is a Bosmer bookseller's client who paid four hundred septims for a pamphlet.

## IV. The Impeccably Correct Embassy

The Summerset Isles closed themselves in the last century, and the government that closed them — the **Thalmor** — maintains in the Imperial City a small legation of eleven staff on the Talos Plaza, whose conduct has been, in the twelve years of its existence, entirely and pointedly beyond reproach. It pays its dues early. It attends every state function and dances badly on purpose. It has never once been caught doing anything.

**First Emissary Ilianwe Tarnaril** presents the Dominion's compliments and asks questions that sound like small talk for eleven years running: which legions rotate through Anvil, which noble houses have Altmeri debt, which of the Emperor's sons has an interest in ships. Her attaché, **Estarion Vaele**, catalogues — literally, in a ledger the Blades have twice failed to steal — every mention of Talos-as-god made in an official Imperial proceeding. Neither has ever said what the ledger is *for*. The embassy's stated position on the Nine Divines is that the Dominion respects the religious customs of its neighbors. It is the word *neighbors* that Jauffre keeps underlining.

*Friction:* Ocato is Altmer, and every meeting he takes at that embassy costs him three votes among the Talos-hardliners. The Thalmor know this. Chroniclers dispute whether they seek him out for information or simply to bleed him. And there is one item nobody will put in writing: the embassy's junior scribe has twice been seen at Phintias's counter, asking after a book that is not for sale.

## V. The Provinces, Which Do Not Consider Themselves Provincial

**Morrowind** governs itself by treaty through its Great Houses, and the treaty is the whole quarrel. **House Hlaalu** grew rich on the Empire's roads and the Empire's law; its councilor **Crassius Curio** — poet, lecher, and by a wide margin the shrewdest man in Balmora — holds Morrowind's Council proxy and sells his vote for tariff exemptions with a frankness the Nibenese find refreshing and the Colovians find obscene. **House Redoran**, under **Archmaster Athyn Sarethi**, keeps the old honor and the ancestor-tombs and considers Hlaalu a house of foreign clerks wearing Dunmer faces; Redoran has begun, this year, refusing Imperial garrison rights at three of its border forts and calling it a scheduling matter. **House Telvanni** does not attend. Its wizard-lords rule from towers by sorcerous strength alone, and its one reform-minded voice, **Master Aryon**, argues that a House which ignores the Empire will be legislated out of existence by clerks it never bothered to meet — an argument his peers find charmingly modern and beside the point. Beneath all three runs the **Camonna Tong**, native-supremacist, skooma-funded, and married to Hlaalu money through **Orvas Dren**, whose brother happens to be the Duke.

**Skyrim** answers to a High King in Solitude, **Rangvar Cold-Crown**, whose authority over the jarls is roughly the authority of a man holding a rope tied to nine dogs. The jarldoms pay Imperial tax and honor Imperial law where the road runs, which in Skyrim is not far. **Jarl Sigdrifa Bear-Widow of Falkreath** holds the Pale Pass road to Bruma and has doubled its toll twice, which Countess Carvain considers a border incident and Sigdrifa considers commerce. **Jarl Kjeld the Unmourned of Windhelm** keeps the Grey Quarter's Dunmer refugees behind a curfew and speaks openly of the Hearth-Oath — the old claim that Morrowind owes Skyrim blood-price for raids two eras settled. The Mages Guild's Skyrim halls operate on sufferance and a great deal of insurance.

**Hammerfell** is three factions pretending to be two. The **Crowns** — the old Na-Totambu bloodlines, strongest in Hegathe and Stros M'Kai, led in argument if not in title by **Rahd al-Marek** — hold that the Redguards conquered Hammerfell before the Empire existed and owe it nothing but courtesy. The **Forebears**, seated in Rihad and Sentinel's ports, hold that the Empire's peace built the docks that feed them; **Admiral Idris al-Rihad** speaks for them at sea and is, not coincidentally, the reason Anvil's pirates retired. Between them, **King Lhotun of Sentinel** has invented a third position — honor the Old Ways *and* the treaty — and the **Lhotunics** are consequently hated by both sides with a purity neither can manage toward the other. Councilor Mena's proposal to grant the Crowns their own Council seat would, everyone agrees, either settle Hammerfell or set it alight.

## VI. The Nine, and Their Weathervanes

The **Imperial Cult** keeps a chapel to the Nine in every city worth the name, and its priests are pastoral counselors, Restoration healers, and — this is the part the chapels do not advertise — the finest intelligence network in Tamriel, since everyone confesses to somebody. **Arch-Prelate Sercia Vellani**, who keeps the Temple of the One in the Imperial City, has spent two years compiling a return she has not yet submitted to the Council: attendance is down in the capital, markedly, among exactly the sort of educated young Nibenese who used to fill her fourth pew. She has asked her deacons where they went. The deacons say: reading.

# The Geography of the Empire, 3E 430

*Being the Fourth Revised Itinerary of the Provinces, compiled for the Imperial Geographical Society, with such corrections as the Society will admit to. Distances given in days of ordinary travel, which is to say the travel of a man with a mule, a road-toll, and no particular enemies.*

---

## I. Cyrodiil, and the Argument Cyrodiil Is Having With Itself

It is convenient for Imperial cartographers to draw Cyrodiil as one province. It is not one province; it is two arguments sharing a road system.

**The Nibenay** — the eastern half, the great warm basin drained by the Niben, whose river is so wide at Bravil that a man on one bank cannot reliably identify a man on the other, and so slow that the barges go downstream by drifting and upstream by cursing. Nibenese Cyrodiils are river people: silk-mad, ancestor-conscious, ruinously fond of ceremony, and comfortable with magic in a way that scandalises their western cousins. They eat rice and lake-fish, they hang paper prayer-lanterns for the Nine at every festival and for three or four things that are certainly not the Nine, and they will describe a family quarrel over a mooring-right in the exact vocabulary the Elder Council uses for a war.

**The Colovian West** — the highlands, the Gold Coast, and the West Weald: dry-stone walls, hard winters, hard wine, and a stubborn provincial memory that the Colovian Estates governed themselves perfectly well before any Septim asked them not to. Colovians pay their taxes, salute the legion, and refer to the Imperial City as *down there*. Chorrol and Skingrad still elect their counts' councils by an old highland formula nobody in the capital has ever bothered to have explained to them.

Between the two lies **the Heartlands** — the flat, wind-combed farm country ringing Lake Rumare, where the wheat is Imperial, the taxes are Imperial, and nobody's grandmother is from anywhere. It is the only part of Cyrodiil that considers itself simply *Imperial*, which is precisely why the Empire keeps its capital in the middle of it.

## II. The Imperial Isle

Lake Rumare is a drowned ring, and the Imperial Isle sits in it like a coin in a bowl. The **White-Gold Tower** rises from the Isle's centre — Ayleid-built, Ayleid-old, and never once improved by anyone since, because nobody has worked out how it stands. Around it the City spirals in wedges: the Green Emperor Way and the Palace at the hub; the Elven Gardens where the old Ayleid foundation-stone still shows through a temple floor; the Talos Plaza District where money lives; the Market District, roofed in awnings, loudest at dawn; the Temple District of the Nine; the Arboretum; and the **Waterfront**, which is technically inside the walls and practically inside no one's jurisdiction at all.

Four bridges cross Rumare — north, south, east, and the great west span past the village of **Weye**, where the Gold Road begins and the Red Ring Road wraps the lake in a slow circuit of ferry-stops, legion mile-forts, and eel-fishers who sell to the Market District at three times what the eels cost in Bravil. The lake's water is warm, green, and full of slaughterfish; the Isle's shoreline is full of Ayleid stonework nobody has permission to dig up and everybody digs up anyway. The Society notes, for the record, that **Varondil** — the flooded Ayleid ruin whose upper arches break the water off the Isle's northern shallows — has been officially sealed on four separate occasions and is presently full of rope.

## III. The Roads, and What They Cost

Cyrodiil's roads are the Empire's proudest boast and its most-audited expense.

- **The Gold Road** runs west from Weye across the Heartlands to the waystation of **Vetter's Rest**, where it forks. The northern branch climbs to **Chorrol** and onward through the Colovian Highlands toward Hammerfell's border passes; the southern branch — the Lower Gold — runs through the vineyard country of **Skingrad**, past hill-perched **Kvatch**, and down to the sea at **Anvil**. Paved, patrolled, tolled at four gates, and still the single most robbed stretch of ground in the province, because a road that carries everything carries everything worth taking.
- **The Silver Road** goes north from the City to **Bruma** through the Great Forest — good stone for two days, then switchbacks, then snow. Closed to wagons for perhaps six weeks most winters, though "closed" in Bruma means the Nords are charging more.
- **The Black Road** runs Chorrol to Bruma along the Jerall foothills. Half of it is legion-laid; the other half is a goat track with pretensions.
- **The Green Road** runs south along the Niben's western bank through **Bravil** to **Leyawiin**. Serviceable, mosquito-plagued below Bravil, and increasingly a formality, since anyone with cargo takes the river.
- **The Blue Road** cuts east to **Cheydinhal** and the Valus passes — the Dunmer road, the one that hears more Dunmeris than Cyrodiilic at its eastern end.
- **The Niben itself** is the best road in Cyrodiil. Barge from the Imperial City to Leyawiin in four days downstream and eight back up, with the poled ferry-hamlet of **Hobb's Ferry** halfway and toll-collectors at every county line.

Off the roads, Cyrodiil stops being tame within a mile. The **Great Forest** hides bandit camps, Ayleid wells, and at least three cults the Legion has stopped counting; the **Imperial Reserve** — the old crown hunting-forest west of the lake, where felling a tree is still technically a capital offence — is a wilderness kept deliberately unmanaged, and everything that wants to be unobserved in central Cyrodiil eventually ends up in it.

## IV. The Frontiers of the Province

**North.** The **Jerall Mountains**, and Bruma clinging to their skirts: black timber halls, turf roofs, Nord surnames on Imperial tax-rolls. Two ways through to Skyrim — the **Bruma Pass**, open five to seven months a year and garrisoned, and **Pale Pass**, which the Empire lists as lost and the smugglers of **Karlholt** list as open, weather permitting, for the right fee.

**East.** The **Valus Mountains**, hot on the Morrowind side and cold on ours, threaded by the Blue Road's climb above Cheydinhal. The eastern descent runs down toward Morrowind's ash country; the caravans that come the other way arrive smelling of ash and carrying ebony they are absolutely not required to declare.

**South.** Below Leyawiin the ground stops agreeing to be ground. **Blackwood** is the transitional swamp where Cyrodiil bleeds into Black Marsh — cypress, black water, ruins with Ayleid arches and something older under them — and the **Panther River** is its main artery, navigable to shallow craft, patrolled by nobody, and the preferred route of every smuggler between Topal Bay and the Marsh. Leyawiin sits at the Niben's mouth on the **Strid**, half Imperial garrison town and half Argonian river-quarter, the two halves observing an armistice of daily inconvenience.

**West.** The **Gold Coast**, where the Abecean comes in warm and Anvil's harbour smells of tar, salt-cod and old piracy. From Anvil's quays you can be in Hammerfell in three days, Summerset in nine, and considerable trouble immediately.

## V. The Ruins Under Everything

Cyrodiil is built on Cyrodiil. **Ayleid** stonework — white, seamless, faintly luminous where the welkynd-stone survives — surfaces in cellars, under temple floors, and in the forest as arches with no building attached: **Varondil** in Lake Rumare, **Tarnwyll** in the West Weald's vine terraces, **Cellaquin** above the Panther River where Blackwood's water runs into the stone and does not come out. They are not merely old ruins; they are the wreckage of the people who owned humans here, and Nibenese farmers still leave them offerings on the reasoning that one should be polite to a bad neighbour.

**Dwemer** ruins are not Cyrodiilic — the Deep Folk built in Morrowind, in Hammerfell's Dragontail range, and under Skyrim's spine — but Dwemer *things* travel: a brass cog in a Bravil pawnshop, a still-humming sphere-frame crated in a Cheydinhal warehouse addressed to no one.

## VI. The Other Provinces, and the Cost of Reaching Them

The Empire is nine provinces and a great deal of water. The Society reminds subscribers that a border on a map is a legal fiction; the real border is the day you stop being able to buy bread with septims and start having to explain yourself.

- **Skyrim** — north over the Jeralls. Jarldoms, mead-halls, barrow-mounds full of draugr that the Nords consider a family matter. The passes shut in winter; the honest route is the Bruma Pass with a Legion writ, and the fast route is Pale Pass with a smuggler and a prayer. Nord courtesy to Imperials is real and thin. Nord courtesy to mages is neither.
- **Morrowind** — east past the Valus, or by ferry from the Imperial City's docks down the Niben and around Topal Bay to **Ebonheart**, twelve to fifteen days if the winds behave. Governed by its own Great House law under treaty, ash-blown, ancestor-haunted, and the only province where an Imperial writ is a suggestion politely received.
- **High Rock** — northwest beyond Hammerfell, or by sea from Anvil to Daggerfall in eight days. A hundred quarrelling Breton duchies and one very complicated recent event that everybody's maps disagree about; two neighbouring lords will hand you two different charts and both will be notarised.
- **Hammerfell** — west out of the Colovian Highlands by the border passes, or three days' sail from Anvil to Stros M'Kai. Alik'r sand, Dragontail crags, and the Crowns-and-Forebears argument in every tavern. Cross the Alik'r without a Redguard guide and the Society respectfully declines responsibility for your remains.
- **Black Marsh** — south of Leyawiin through Blackwood, and no further than that if you value your lungs. The Empire has never mapped the interior; the roads that exist were made by the Marsh and can be unmade by it. Argonian settlements answer to the Hist, which is a word Imperial administrators write down and do not understand.
- **Elsweyr** — south-west, reached overland from Bravil and Leyawiin through the border scrub, or by ship into Topal Bay to Senchal. Sand in the north, savanna and sugarcane in the south, and caravan-clans whose routes are older than the roads they cross. The moon-sugar trade is legal in Elsweyr, taxed in Cyrodiil, and hanged for in Skyrim.
- **Valenwood** — west of Elsweyr, a forest that is in some measure alive. The Green Pact forbids the Bosmer to harm plant life on their own soil, so their roads are trodden, not cut, and a hostile Valenwood is a province with no roads at all. Thalmor "advisors" appear at more Bosmer courts each year, which the Elder Council has noticed and has decided to notice quietly.
- **Summerset Isles** — across the Abecean, and effectively shut. Altmer harbour-writs are required, granted rarely, and revoked without explanation. What Imperial traders see of Summerset is Firsthold's outer mole and a very polite official copying down their names twice.

*Postscript, added by the compiler in a smaller hand:* the Society's charts of Cyrodiil are excellent, of Hammerfell adequate, of Black Marsh imaginative, and of Summerset entirely copied from a chart of 3E 401. This is not carelessness. It is the shape of the Empire's reach, drawn honestly for once.

# A History of the Empire, Abridged for Persons of Ordinary Patience

*Being the fourth revision of the standing chronology maintained by the Imperial Geographical Society, with such corrections as the Elder Council has permitted, and such rumours as this compiler could not bring himself to omit.*
— Brother Aldos Venim, cloister-scribe, 3E 430

---

## I. On the Problem of Dates

Before any history of Tamriel can be told, an honest chronicler must confess that Tamriel does not agree with itself about when anything happened.

The **Septim Count** — the official Imperial reckoning, promulgated from the White-Gold Tower and taught in every Cyrodiilic schoolroom — dates the crowning of Talos, called Tiber Septim, to **1E 2321**, and holds the Third Empire to be the direct and unbroken continuation of that coronation. The **Amber Roll**, which is the Elder Council's own session-ledger and has never once missed a sitting, records only some four centuries of Councils convened under the Septim seal. Both documents are kept in the same building. Neither has been permitted to correct the other since the Society formally declined, in 3E 404, to adjudicate between them.

The reason offered — quietly, and never in a public volume — is that time in Tamriel has *broken* before, and demonstrably did so again within living memory during the Warp in the West. Where the Dragon has broken, the years on either side of the break do not reconcile, and men who lived through it remember several different true things. The Society's official position is that the discrepancy is clerical. The Society's archivists, over wine, say otherwise.

The practical Imperial citizen resolves this the way Imperial citizens resolve everything: he uses whichever date his tax-writ uses, and does not raise the matter at dinner.

## II. The Merethic Era — Which Is Myth, and Which the Temples Insist Is Not

Before years were counted there were the **Ehlnofey**, the Earth Bones, who were the first things that were not gods and not yet men, and who broke themselves into the shape of the world in a war nobody has ever satisfactorily explained. From their sundering came two inheritances: the **Aldmer**, who remembered their divine parentage and grieved it, and the **Nedes**, who forgot and were content.

The Aldmer came out of the drowned Aldmeris to **Summerset**, and from Summerset spread. Those who went east into the swamps became **Ayleid** (rendered *Aylied* in the older Colovian rolls), and raised white-stone towers across the Heartlands, and lit them with stars, and kept human slaves in numbers no Imperial history quite states aloud. Those who went underground became **Dwemer** and stopped speaking to anyone. Those who followed the prophet **Veloth** east across the Velothi Mountains became **Chimer**, and would one day become **Dunmer**, and have not forgiven anybody for anything since.

North, out of frozen Atmora, came **Ysgramor** and the Five Hundred Companions, who found Skyrim already inhabited by the snow-elves and made it uninhabited by them. Every Nord grudge in the present day is a footnote to that voyage, and Nords consider this a virtue.

## III. The First Era — Where the Provinces Get Their Shapes

**c. 1E 242 — The Slave Rebellion.** The Ayleid dominion of Cyrodiil ended when a slave called **Alessia** raised the Nedic bondsmen of the Heartlands against their star-lit masters, and — this is the load-bearing part — bargained the Nords into helping. Her victory founded the first human Empire and, more consequentially, the compact of the **Eight Divines**: Aedra worship rendered into state religion, a settlement between human faith and elven pantheon that every Imperial temple still runs on. The Nine came later, when Talos was added to their number, and the Aldmeri have never once said the word "Nine" without a small pause before it.

**1E 361 onward — The Alessian Order.** Alessia's faith curdled into the Alessian Order, whose reforms were sufficiently intolerable that they eventually produced the **War of Righteousness**, in which the Empire's priests destroyed one another with such thoroughness that the Empire itself fell over. First lesson of Imperial history: the Empire has never been destroyed by an enemy. It has only ever been destroyed by its own devout.

**1E 700 — The Disappearance.** At Red Mountain, in a single unrecorded instant, the entire Dwemer race ceased to be present in the world. Their cities remain, lit, warm, and running. Their machines remain, and still work, and still kill intruders with the patience of things that were never alive. No Aedric or Daedric explanation has ever fit, which is precisely what makes the ruins so unpopular with theologians and so popular with adventurers. In the same conflict the Chimer were changed — ash-skinned, red-eyed — and three of their number ascended as the **Tribunal**, living gods to whom Morrowind prayed for something over three thousand years.

**1E 808 — The Yokudan Landing.** The Ra Gada came out of the drowned continent of Yokuda in fleets, took the peninsula that is now **Hammerfell**, and immediately split into the **Crowns**, who held that the old kingship and the old ways came with them, and the **Forebears**, who held that the new land deserved new terms. That argument is one thousand six hundred years old and is still, as of this morning, an argument.

**1E 2703 — Akavir.** The Akaviri invasion was broken at Pale Pass by **Reman Cyrodiil**, whereupon the surviving invaders knelt to the man who had beaten them and became his bodyguard. They are the ancestors of the **Blades**. This is the Second Empire's founding, and it is the reason the Emperor's personal guard has always been recruited from outsiders, and why the Blades' loyalty runs to the *Dragonborn* rather than to the Council.

## IV. The Second Era — The Long Silence

The Reman line ended in murder, and Tamriel spent some four hundred years without a centre. Provinces went their own way and remember it fondly. The **Knahaten Flu** emptied Black Marsh's borderlands and killed almost no Argonians, which the rest of Tamriel noticed and has never stopped noticing. Three great alliances — Daggerfall's Covenant, the Ebonheart Pact, and the first **Aldmeri Dominion** — fought over the ruby throne and left behind the durable idea that Summerset may legitimately claim to rule Tamriel. The present Thalmor did not invent that claim. They inherited it, and they have the paperwork.

Then a Colovian warlord called **Hjalti Early-Beard**, later **Talos**, later **Tiber Septim**, took Cyrodiil, then Skyrim, then everything, ending with the **Armistice** that brought Morrowind into the Empire without conquering it — the Great Houses keeping their law, their Temple, and their internal rule in exchange for tribute and legions. That treaty is why House Redoran answers to a Councilor and not a Governor, and why Morrowind remains, four centuries on, the Empire's most fragile client. Tiber Septim is also held to have wielded the brass god **Numidium** in that campaign. The Imperial histories mention it in one line. The Altmeri histories do not stop mentioning it.

Talos was made a god after his death — the Ninth Divine — and the Aldmeri consider this the single greatest blasphemy in recorded history. Every polite exchange between the Elder Council and the Thalmor embassy is conducted over that fact, in the way one conducts a dinner over a body under the table.

## V. The Third Era — The Septim Centuries

The Septim line produced saints, administrators, and at least one Emperor (**Pelagius III**) who was mad enough to become a Daedric attraction. The **War of the Red Diamond** taught the Council that the line can fracture and the Empire survive. It also taught them the cost, which was a generation.

**3E 389–399 — The Imperial Simulacrum.** The Imperial Battlemage **Jagar Tharn** imprisoned Uriel Septim VII outside the world and wore his face for ten years. He governed adequately, which is the detail nobody likes. Provinces were taxed, writs were sealed, the Council convened, and no Councilor noticed. It is the founding trauma of modern Imperial politics: *the Elder Council can be run for a decade by something that is not the Emperor, and not know.* Every succession faction in the present day traces its paranoia to those ten years.

**3E 397–399 — The Camoran Usurper.** While a Battlemage wore the Emperor's face and let the west burn, a claimant of the old Camoran line came north out of Valenwood with a host that did not require food, water, or burial, and took most of the Iliac coast. He was broken at last by a coalition under Baron Othrok at the **Battle of Firewaves**. It remains the last serious armed challenge to the Septim line — and, more usefully for the present chronicler, the last time Tamriel saw a Daedra-fed army in the field. Veterans of that campaign are in their sixties now, and when they see a summoned thing they do not react like other men.

**3E 403 — The Warp in the West.** Something happened at the Iliac Bay. Forty-four squabbling Breton and Redguard kingdoms became four — Daggerfall, Wayrest, Sentinel, and Orsinium — in the space of a day, or a week, or several contradictory years, depending on whose survivor you ask, and every survivor is telling the truth. The Empire's official position is that the Miracle of Peace was a diplomatic settlement of the War of Betony. Everyone who was standing in High Rock that day knows better and has learned not to say so in front of Imperial clerks. The Warp is the reason the Society will no longer adjudicate a date, and the reason a certain kind of Breton is very hard to interrogate.

**3E ~415 — The Mythic Dawn.** A small cult begins meeting in the Imperial City's cellars, distributing four slim volumes of *Commentaries* to men of means and grievances. It teaches that the Empire's peace is a cage built by the Divines, that the Nine are jailers, and that a Prince's chosen champion could unbar the door. The Elder Council's Office of Sanctions logged it in 3E 416 as a philosophical society. That entry has never been amended.

## VI. The Quiet Decade and What Followed — 3E 415 to 430

Uriel Septim VII came back from Tharn's captivity in 399 and has never entirely come back. He is over eighty. He is sane, courteous, and enormously tired, and he has three living sons — **Geldall**, **Enman**, and **Ebel** — each of whom is the favoured instrument of a different Council bloc, and none of whom the Emperor has formally confirmed. This is not an oversight. Uriel has been asked, in Council, four times in eleven years, and has each time changed the subject with a grace that has stopped anyone asking a fifth. High Chancellor **Ocato** has permitted precisely one written record of these refusals, and it is not in the Amber Roll.

What has visibly changed since 415, in the order an ordinary subject would notice it:

- **The roads.** The Legion never fully restaffed the Colovian garrisons emptied during the Simulacrum. The **Gold Road** is patrolled at half the strength the tax-writs claim, and every Chorrol timber-factor now hires his own escort. Banditry is not rebellion, but it is the shape rebellion takes first.
- **The Jerall Tithe Refusal (3E 419).** Two Nord jarls declined the legion levy, citing an Old Ways precedent four centuries stale. Bruma's Count negotiated instead of arresting, and the Council ratified the negotiation. Every province read that correctly.
- **The Rihad Grain Writ (3E 421).** A Forebears-appointed harbour-master applied an Imperial grain tariff to Crown-held estates. The riot lasted three days, killed forty, and ended when the Legion cohort declined to deploy. Hammerfell's schism has been an argument for sixteen centuries; in 421 it briefly became an arithmetic problem about how many Redguards the Empire could actually compel.
- **The Blackwood Levy (3E 423).** Leyawiin's garrison conscripted Argonian labourers from the Blackwood under a corvée statute nobody had invoked in a hundred years. Something in Black Marsh began organising in response. Imperial intelligence on it is, in the Council's own phrasing, "sufficient for present purposes," which is a phrase the Council uses when it has none.
- **The Silence of the Silvenar (3E 424).** Valenwood's spirit-voice ceased answering Imperial correspondence. The Thalmor embassy expressed, with impeccable courtesy, its regret. Bosmer timber and Bosmer archers have both grown noticeably harder to hire in the Empire since.
- **The Anvil Manifests (3E 426).** Anvil's harbour rolls record Summerset-flagged hulls calling at the Gold Coast under third-party charter at four times the rate of a decade ago. Nothing about this is illegal. Every one of them departs with copies of Imperial provincial censuses, which are also not illegal, and are also not usually interesting to merchants.
- **The Tribunal's Ending (3E 427).** In Morrowind the Blight broke, Red Mountain went quiet, and the living gods of the Tribunal simply — stopped. The Temple reconstituted itself as the New Temple and now preaches the sainthood of what it once worshipped as divinity. House Hlaalu, which had bet everything on Imperial goodwill, is bleeding influence; House Redoran is ascendant and remembers exactly whose peace this was. Dunmer refugees have been arriving in Cyrodiil ever since, and Cyrodiil has been enjoying them as much as Cyrodiil ever enjoys anything.
- **The Chorrol Tally.** The Fighters Guild's own contract ledger, which the Guild does not publish, shows that in 415 roughly one contract in thirty involved Daedric summoning, conjured vermin, or shrine activity. By 429 it is closer to one in seven. Guild pay for those contracts has not risen accordingly, which is why Chorrol's veterans are angry about a thing they have not yet correctly identified.

And what the Council has papered over:

- **The Sancre Tor Quiet (3E 418).** A Blades detachment was dispatched to Tiber Septim's tomb-fortress on business the Amber Roll describes as "restoration of a monument." Nine went in. Four came out. The stonemasons' contract was never issued.
- **The Redacted Session (3E 428).** The Amber Roll's entry for the eleventh session of that year consists of a date, an attendance list, and eleven inches of blank vellum, ruled but unwritten. Chancellor Ocato has confirmed on the record that the session occurred. Attendance included two Blades officers, which no ordinary session ever does.
- **The Rumare Lights (3E 429 onward).** Watch reports from the Imperial Isle describe a red cast to the sky over Lake Rumare at odd hours, unaccompanied by weather. The Imperial Astronomer's office has classified them as aurora, which is a category that does not previously appear in five centuries of Cyrodiilic watch-books.
- **The Emperor's Watches.** The palace staff, who are discreet and therefore reliable, report that Uriel Septim VII no longer sleeps a full night, and has not since 428. He has doubled his Blade escort without stating a cause. He has attended fewer public rites each year, and at the last three he did not wear the Amulet of Kings. The priests of the Temple of the One state that the Dragonfires burn as they have always burned. They would say that in either case.

It is the position of this compiler, offered without the Society's endorsement, that the Third Empire in 3E 430 is not sick. It is *held*. Held by an old man's habits, a Chancellor's paperwork, and four centuries of everyone agreeing to behave as though the arrangement is permanent. Such things hold beautifully, and for a long time, and then they hold no longer, and the histories written afterward always note that the signs were everywhere.

They are everywhere.

# On the Arts Arcane in the Year 3E 430

*Being a compilation drawn from the lecture-notes of the Arcane University, the
Imperial Cult's catechisms, and certain almanacs the Council would prefer went
uncopied. Compiled for the Imperial Geographical Society; errors are the
compiler's, and there are certainly errors.*

---

## I. The Two Lenders

It is the first thing taught at the Arcane University and the first thing every
apprentice forgets: **no one makes magic.** Magicka is borrowed, and there are
only two houses in Tamriel that lend it.

The **Aedra** — the Eight, and the Ninth whom the Empire added and the Altmer
resent — gave their substance to make Mundus and were diminished by the giving.
Aedric power is therefore *finite, structural, and honest*. It heals, it binds,
it holds a shape in place. It behaves the same way on Loredas as it did on
Morndas. It has no personality, makes no offers, and cannot be bargained with,
because the Aedra spent themselves in the Convention and have very little left
to spend. When an Imperial Cult priest closes a wound in the Chapel of Dibella,
the doctrine holds that the strength came out of the world's own founding
capital — which is why the Cult teaches that healing is a *loan against
creation*, why chapels tithe, and why an old priest's hands shake. The Nine do
not send collectors. The account simply runs down.

The **Daedra** — the sixteen-odd Princes of the realms of Oblivion — refused
the Convention. They kept everything. Daedric power is therefore *infinite,
immediate, personal, and lent at interest*. A Prince can give a nineteen-year-old
crofter's daughter more raw force in one evening than the Arcane University
could teach her in twenty years, and will, cheerfully, if she is interesting.
The Princes do not punish, exactly. They **collect** — on their own schedule,
in their own coin, and almost never the coin the petitioner offered. The
scholar Cavillius Rell puts it best in his *Praxis of the Unequal Contract*:
"Aedric magic asks what you can afford. Daedric magic asks what you are for."

A working caster in 3E 430 uses both and pretends otherwise. Every Mages Guild
hall in Cyrodiil teaches Conjuration, which is Daedric by definition, under an
Imperial charter signed beneath a ceiling painted with the Nine. This
hypocrisy is so old it has its own vocabulary: guild-licensed summoning is
called **"borrowing under seal,"** and the seal is a Mysticism binding that
returns the creature before it can develop opinions.

## II. Magicka, Magnus, and the Hole in the Sky

Magicka is the residue of the architect's departure. Magnus, who drew the plans
for Mundus and then would not pay for them, fled creation and tore the sky
getting out; that tear is the sun. The lesser Magna-Ge went with him and made
the stars, which are lesser holes. Through all of them, light-that-is-not-light
leaks in, and that leakage is what a mage draws on.

Practical consequences, as taught in the first-year lecture at the Arcane
University:

- Magicka is thicker where the veil is thin — Ayleid ruins, old battlefields,
  and any ground a Prince has stood on personally.
- **Welkynd stones** (Ayleid pale-blue) hold ambient magicka; **varla stones**
  (white, rarer) hold enough to fill a soul gem. Both are illegal to remove
  from Imperial Reserve sites and both are removed constantly.
- Dwemer ruins are *dead ground* — the machinery down there runs on no magicka
  at all and the Guild has never gotten a satisfying answer as to what it does
  run on. Enchanters hate working near animunculi. Nobody knows why.

## III. The Six Schools, as the Guild Teaches Them

The Mages Guild's charter obliges it to teach any qualified applicant
regardless of birth, province, or race — the Empire's great equalizer, and the
reason conservative Skyrim jarls and Redoran councilors spit when they say
"the College." Instruction is organised into six schools. (The older Daggerfall
curriculum had a seventh, **Thaumaturgy**; the Guild dissolved it a century ago
and split its corpus between Alteration and Mysticism. Breton hedge-wizards in
High Rock still use the word and still resent the Guild for taking it.)

**Alteration** — the school of *insisting*. It does not create; it argues with
what already exists until the world concedes. Water becomes walkable, a lock
becomes an open lock, skin becomes stone. Alteration is the most Aedric of the
practical schools because it works entirely on Mundus's own rules and never
asks anything from Oblivion. It is also the most *tiring*, and the most
academic: an Alteration master is expected to publish. The Guild's Alteration
faculty are insufferable about this.

**Conjuration** — the school of *borrowing bodies*. Two branches: summoning
(a scamp, a clannfear, a dremora, a bound sword that is really a dremora's
sword lent for ninety seconds) and — formerly — raising (the dead). Everything
Conjuration touches belongs to somebody. Guild doctrine holds that a summoned
Daedra is *rented, not commanded*, and that it goes home able to describe you.
The second branch is the Guild's live wound: **Arch-Mage Hannibal Traven, newly
seated at the Arcane University, has banned the practice of necromancy
outright.** The ban is two years old, deeply unpopular with the Guild's older
faculty, and has quietly emptied several halls into the arms of practitioners
who no longer need to pretend. Traven has made enemies he cannot name, and
knows it.

**Destruction** — the school of *spending*. Fire, frost, shock, and the
weakening curses that make all three land harder. The easiest school to learn
badly and the fastest to make a peasant dangerous, which is exactly why Nord
tradition despises it and Imperial legions field battlemages anyway.
Destruction is taught with a discipline the other schools find theatrical:
Guild apprentices spend their first season not casting at all, only holding a
flame at a fixed size for an hour. Those who cannot are sent to Alteration.

**Illusion** — the school of *editing the witness*. Light, silence, charm,
courage, fear, frenzy, invisibility. Officially the gentlest school; in
practice the one the Elder Council most wants licensed, because a competent
Illusionist in a council chamber is worth three assassins. Its dark
half — the compulsions that rewrite a mind rather than merely dress it — sits
uncomfortably close to the gifts of Vaermina and Mephala, and the Guild's
charter forbids casting Charm on an Imperial officeholder. That prohibition is
violated roughly weekly.

**Mysticism** — the school of *the rules themselves*. Detection, telekinesis,
soul trapping, dispelling, reflection, and the binding-seals that make licensed
summoning survivable. It is the least glamorous school and the one the
Guild would collapse without: Mysticism wrote the wards on the Arcane
University's walls, the soul gem, and the contract-seal that keeps a dremora
polite. Mysticists are the profession's lawyers, and are regarded accordingly.
Magister Cavillius Rell, who holds the Mysticism chair, has spent three years
measuring what he calls "the thinning" — a slow, unglamorous drift in the
resistance between Mundus and Oblivion — and cannot get the Council to read
his paper.

**Restoration** — the school of *paying back*. Healing, curing, fortifying,
warding, and turning undead. The one school that is unambiguously Aedric, the
one every hall teaches free of charge to any applicant, and the one that makes
the Guild tolerable to the Imperial Cult. A Restoration adept and a Cult priest
do the same work by different liturgies and both know it; they are polite to
each other in public and drink together in private.

**Alchemy and Enchanting** are trades, not schools — the Guild's Lustratorium
teaches the first, the Chironasium the second, and the Praxographical Center
lets ranked members compose original spells for a fee. All three are how a
guild mage actually earns septims.

### Guild standing is a resource

Rank in the Guild — Associate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Evoker, Conjurer,
Magician, Warlock, Wizard, Master-Wizard, Arch-Mage — buys library access,
Praxographical time, letters of introduction, and legal cover. Expulsion costs
all of it, and an expelled mage in Cyrodiil is a mage the Legion is entitled to
question. This is leverage, and everyone from the Elder Council to the Mythic
Dawn knows it.

## IV. The Nine Divines and Their Provincial Heresies

The Imperial Cult keeps chapels in every major city: **Akatosh** (time,
endurance, the Emperor's own patron and the Dragonfire's warrant), **Arkay**
(the cycle of birth and death — his priests are why necromancy is a *crime*
and not merely a scandal), **Dibella** (beauty), **Julianos** (logic and
lore — the Cult's own scholars, and the Guild's closest allies in the Cult),
**Kynareth** (sky, storm, wild places), **Mara** (love, the household),
**Stendarr** (mercy, and the militant compassion of the Vigil), **Zenithar**
(work, trade, honest coin), and **Talos**, who was Tiber Septim, who was a man,
and who is the ninth. The Altmer of Summerset consider that last claim
obscene, and the Thalmor's embassy in the Imperial City is *scrupulously*
correct about never quite saying so aloud.

Aedric blessing is not spellcasting. It is petition. A priest asks, and the
answer is the same reliable trickle it was yesterday. This is why Cult healing
never fails spectacularly and never exceeds itself either.

The provinces keep their own arrangements. Morrowind's Dunmer venerate the
**Tribunal** — Almalexia, Sotha Sil, Vivec, three living gods the Imperial Cult
tolerates through gritted teeth by treaty — over and above the **ancestors**,
whose ash-statues sit in every Redoran hall. The Bosmer answer to **Y'ffre**
the Storyteller, whose Green Pact forbids harming Valenwood's own trees and
beasts. The Redguards remember **Ruptga** and the Walkabout, and their Ansei
sword-singers hold that a blade held correctly is a prayer. The Nords swear by
**Shor** and by Talos-the-man rather than Talos-the-god, and regard the whole
Cyrodiilic pantheon as a filing system imposed by clerks.

## V. The Princes of Oblivion

Sixteen shrines of note stand in the Cyrodiilic wilderness alone, most of them
half a day off any road, each attended by a handful of devotees who will tell
you they are not a cult. Each Prince may be petitioned only on their own day,
with their own offering, and the almanacs disagree about both.

**Mehrunes Dagon** — *Destruction, Change, Ambition, Revolution.* Summoned the
20th of Sun's Dusk; his petitioners bring a Daedra heart and the will to burn
something worth burning. Dagon does not want worshippers, he wants *events*. He
offers his champions the pleasure of being the hinge history turns on, and asks
in return that they never stop turning it. He is patient exactly as long as a
plan needs him to be. The Mythic Dawn are his, though most of Cyrodiil has not
yet heard the name of either.

**Molag Bal** — *Domination, Enslavement, the Corruption of Mortal Souls.*
Summoned the 20th of Evening Star. The King of Rape wants what he has always
wanted: not your service but your *ownership*, and preferably a chain you
fastened yourself. His bargains are generous, explicit, and always contain a
clause about obedience that the petitioner reads as ceremonial. It is not
ceremonial. Vampirism in most of Tamriel is his fingerprint.

**Sheogorath** — *Madness.* Summoned the 2nd of Sun's Height with a lettuce, a
yarn, and a lesser soul gem, which is either a joke or a precise ritual
requirement, and the difference has cost lives. He gives extravagantly and
without conditions, then rearranges the recipient. He is the only Prince who
tells you the truth at the moment you cannot use it. Court wisdom holds him
harmless. Court wisdom has not met him.

**Azura** — *Dusk and Dawn, the Magic Between, Prophecy.* Summoned at
Hogithum, the 21st of First Seed, with glow dust gathered at twilight. The most
courteous of the Princes and the most dangerous to disappoint: Azura keeps her
word to the letter and expects the same, and her curses are famously
*hereditary*. Half of Morrowind's Dunmer keep her shrine, insisting she is not
a Daedra in the way the others are. She wants foresight used well, and remembers
petitioners across generations.

**Boethiah** — *Plots, Betrayal, Murder in Good Cause, Rightful Rule by
Strength.* Summoned the 2nd of Sun's Dawn with the blood of a worthy foe. She
does not accept petitioners; she accepts *victors*. Her price is always the
same and always paid to her next favourite: to keep Boethiah's gift you must
remain the strongest thing that wants it. Dunmer of House Redoran and Telvanni
both quietly honour her, which tells you a great deal about Morrowind.

**Hermaeus Mora** — *Knowledge, Memory, the Fates of the Unseen.* Summoned the
5th of First Seed with an offering of writing no one else has read. He never
threatens and never refuses. He simply answers, completely, and each answer
costs a piece of the asker they will not notice missing until they need it. His
apocrypha circulate through the Arcane University's restricted stacks by
routes the librarians pretend to control. Traven's necromancy ban has driven at
least one very good scholar into Mora's arms this season.

**Clavicus Vile** — *Wishes, Bargains, Power Bought Cheap.* Summoned the 1st of
Morning Star with a jewel worth a thousand septims and a straight face. The
friendliest Prince and the worst deal in Oblivion: Vile grants exactly what was
asked for, in the wording used, and enjoys the aftermath enormously. His hound
Barbas is often the more honest party. He is the Prince most likely to be
encountered as a *middleman* — a smiling stranger in an Imperial City tavern
offering to fix a problem for something you have plenty of.

**Nocturnal** — *Night, Darkness, Luck, What Is Taken and Not Returned.*
Summoned the 3rd of Hearthfire. She asks for nothing and offers nothing in
words; her gift arrives as luck that has already begun. The Thieves Guild's
oldest oaths bend toward her without ever naming her, and every fence in Anvil
touches wood at the Grey Fox's name. Her collection, when it comes, is
usually a *shadow* — some part of a life that afterwards no one, including its
owner, can quite recall.

*Also honoured, more quietly:* **Mephala** (secrets, sex, murder — the
Morag Tong's patron and Mora's rival in the business of knowing things),
**Hircine** (the hunt; every werecreature in Tamriel is his livestock),
**Vaermina** (nightmares and stolen memory — the Illusion faculty's private
terror), **Malacath** (the sworn oath and the spurned; the Orsimer's own),
**Sanguine** (debauchery, and the cheapest bargain a fool ever regretted),
**Meridia** (life-energies and a loathing of the undead so absolute she is
sometimes mistaken for an Aedra, which infuriates her), and **Peryite**
(pestilence and the tidy ordering of the lowest things).

**Sithis** the Imperial Cult files among the Princes for want of a better
shelf; the Dark Brotherhood's own scripture insists he is older than the shelf,
older than the Princes, and not a *he*. The Brotherhood does not argue the
point with outsiders. It sends someone instead.

## VI. How a Bargain Actually Goes

The Guild teaches four stages, and warns that a petitioner never notices which
one they are in.

1. **Notice.** You are seen. Something goes right that shouldn't have.
2. **Gift.** A small, useful, unasked-for thing. A blade, a dream, a name. No
   terms are stated. Stating terms is a mortal habit.
3. **Use.** You spend the gift. Each use is a signature.
4. **Collection.** Never what was offered. Always what you turned out to be
   *for*.

There is no ritual of refusal, only of return, and the return costs more than
the gift was worth. This is not a tax. It is a **story debt**, and the world
will decide when it is due.

## VII. Birthsigns

Every Tamrielic child is born under one of the thirteen constellations, and
Tamriel's people take this as seriously as parentage. The three Guardians — the
**Warrior**, the **Mage**, the **Thief** — and their charges (the Lady, the
Steed, the Lord; the Apprentice, the Atronach, the Ritual; the Lover, the
Shadow, the Tower), plus the **Serpent**, which wanders and portends nothing
good. A Bruma midwife will name the sign before she names the child. Two facts
the Guild considers established: those born under the **Atronach** hold more
magicka than they can generate and must feed on spells cast at them, and those
born under the **Serpent** are refused apprenticeship by at least two Cyrodiilic
halls on grounds no charter permits and every faculty defends.

## VIII. The Same Magic, Different Schooling — a Note for Practitioners

Tamriel has no "wizards" in the abstract. It has *credentials*.

- A **Battlemage** is Destruction and Alteration, Legion-trained, and wears
  what a legionary wears.
- A **Spellsword** is the same two schools with a Fighters Guild contract
  instead of a Legion commission.
- A **Nightblade** is Illusion and Destruction, self-taught or Guild-expelled,
  and no one asks which.
- A **Healer** or Cult **priest** is Restoration and Alteration with the Nine's
  liturgy over the top; their power is petition, not study.
- A **Conjurer** is licensed by seal and watched by Mysticism.
- A **Sorcerer** is a Breton or a Prince-pacted mortal whose magicka came with
  their blood or their bargain rather than a curriculum — and the Guild's
  admission board can never quite decide how to rank them.
- A **Witchhunter** is Restoration and Mysticism turned outward, usually
  Stendarr's, usually humourless.
- A **Sword-Singer** of Hammerfell holds that none of the above is magic at all,
  and produces evidence.

The question a Tamrielic caster is asked at a city gate is never *what can you
do*. It is **who taught you, and are you still in good standing with them.**

# Technology — Dwemer Artifice

*Being a brief and necessarily incomplete account of the Dwarven works, as compiled for the Imperial Geographical Society, 3E 430.*

## The Deep Folk and Their Absence

It is held — and the Imperial Commission on Dwemer Studies has spent four decades failing to hold otherwise — that the Dwemer were not dwarves at all, but a heresy of Mer: elves who studied the world the way a surgeon studies a body, and who, at some hour of the year 1E 700, in the deeps beneath Red Mountain, stopped being anywhere. Not slain. Not scattered. *Absent*, all of them, in every hall from Vvardenfell to the Alik'r, in a single indivisible instant — their tools still warm, their soup still on the boil, their lamps still lit. Every scholar of the Third Empire has a theory. The Telvanni say they unmade themselves on purpose. The Redoran say Azura took them for an old insult. The Commission's own official position, printed at Council expense, is four hundred pages long and concludes that further study is warranted. **No one knows. Nothing found in any ruin has ever said.** Treat any NPC who claims certainty as a liar, a madman, or a cultist — sometimes all three.

What they left behind is the point. Dwemer artifice does not answer to the Aedra or the Daedra. It is not enchanted; the Arcane University has confirmed this to its considerable irritation, since a Mysticism adept can read the weave of a Nord grave-hex from across a room and reads *nothing at all* off a Dwarven lamp that has been burning without fuel since before the First Empire. The machinery is cold, mathematical, and indifferent. It ran before the Empire and it will run after. That indifference is the horror of the ruins: not malice, but the total absence of any interest in you.

## The Ruins

There are no Dwemer ruins in Cyrodiil. The Heartlands' bones are Ayleid — white stone and welkynd glass — and a Colovian farmer who tells you he ploughed up "a dwarf hall" has ploughed up an elf cellar. Dwarven halls lie elsewhere: thick under Morrowind's Velothi range and Vvardenfell's ash (**Arkngthand**, **Nchuleftingth**, **Bthungthumz**), through Skyrim's under-mountain (**Mzinchaleft**, **Alftand**, and the half-drowned **Mzeneldt** on the Skyrim shoulder of the Bruma pass), and sunk in Hammerfell's sand at **Volenfell**. Cyrodiil gets the *cargo*, not the ruin — which is precisely why the Imperial City is the best market in Tamriel for Dwarven scrap and the worst place in Tamriel to be caught carrying it unlicensed.

A ruin is not a dungeon that has gone quiet. It is a machine that has been running unsupervised for seventeen centuries: pistons still stroking behind the walls, steam still venting on a schedule nobody set, a great brass sun-clock in a flooded gallery still turning to mark hours in a calendar the world no longer keeps. Doors open when the mechanism decides they open. Bridges extend for reasons. Somewhere below, water is being pumped somewhere, for something.

## Animunculi

The ruins keep their own staff. **Spiders** — knee-high, brass, quick, and swarming — are maintenance: they come for the intrusion the way a house comes for a rat, and they are the ones that kill careless first-timers. **Spheres** travel folded into a ball down the corridors and unfold mid-roll into a bladed thing with one arm and one crossbow, which is exactly as unpleasant to meet in a stairwell as it sounds. **Centurions**, ten feet of steam and hammer-armed bronze, stand in their alcoves venting quietly for centuries and step down when a threshold is crossed — and a party that has cheerfully carved through forty spiders should be told, plainly and in advance, that a centurion is a different order of problem. **Steam centurions** are worse and rarer. There is talk, mostly Telvanni talk, of things further down that are neither.

None of them are alive. None of them are undead. Conjuration does not bind them, Illusion does not fool them, turning does nothing; they have no mind to frighten and no soul to leverage. They are also not *guards* in any sense a mortal would recognize — they do not pursue past a boundary, do not coordinate, do not care who you are or what you took, and will ignore a thief standing perfectly still in a doorway while shredding his friend three feet away. Their logic is real, consistent, and unpublished. Players who watch carefully can learn a ruin's rules. Players who assume the rules are the same as the last ruin's die.

## Loot, Hazard, Wonder — Never a Tech Tree

Nobody in 3E 430 builds this. Nobody repairs it. The most a mortal smith achieves is *reforging*: melting Dwarven metal — that dull warm gold-brown alloy nobody has reproduced — into armor and blades that are lighter and better than steel and utterly mundane in every other way. A handful of Dunmer scrapsmiths and one or two Orsimer in the Empire can work it at all; everyone else spoils the metal and sells the ruin of it as curiosity.

So Dwemer things enter play as three faces of the same object:
- **Loot.** Cogs, gears, struts, and dynamo cores fetch real septims from Commission agents, Mages Guild researchers, and collectors who do not ask about permits. A centurion's dynamo core is a small fortune and heavy enough to make you choose between it and the exit.
- **Hazard.** Steam vents, ceiling-mounted blade-arms, a lift that answers a lever after a six-second pause, and animunculi that wake on a trigger the party will never identify. The trap is not a trap; it is a machine doing its job with you in the way.
- **Wonder.** A gallery of brass reeds the height of a cathedral that hums when you shout in it. A wall of etched Dwemeris that the finest translator in the Empire can render as *"— and therefore the twelfth is inadmissible —"* and no more. An unbroken lamp. These beats should land as awe, not as a puzzle to solve.

Give the players Dwemer artifice as an *encounter with something older and better than them that does not acknowledge them*. Let them take metal from it. Never let them master it, and never — under any circumstance, however clever the question — let the GM explain where the Dwemer went.

## The Law of It

The Elder Council's **Antiquities Statute** makes every Dwemer site Imperial property and every unlicensed excavation a crime, prosecuted by the **Imperial Commission on Dwemer Studies** — a body with more clerks than diggers and an inspector's writ that outranks most provincial magistrates. In Morrowind this collides with Great House law: Hlaalu sells permits it doesn't hold, Redoran considers the whole enterprise grave-robbing regardless of who signed what, and Telvanni excavate whatever they please and dare the Commission to come say so. The paperwork is a genuine adventure obstacle. So is the fence who can make it unnecessary.
