# Culture — Larenne, and What He Carries Into It

## I. THE VISIBLE WORLD: BRETON LIFE IN A MOORLAND MARKET TOWN

### The shape of a day

Larenne keeps its hours by the Chapterhouse bells, and has for longer than anyone
troubles to say. There are four that matter. **Firstlight** is rung while the sky is
still the colour of dishwater, and it is less a summons to prayer than a permission
to make noise: after Firstlight a man may split wood, beat a rug, or shout at his
own children without being thought ill-bred. The **Trade Toll** comes mid-morning and
opens the market cross — before it, no bargain struck in Larenne is legally a
bargain, which is why so many of them are struck at a quarter to. The **Ash Bell** at
dusk is the one people actually love. It means hearths are banked, doors are shut,
and whatever you have not finished today has stopped being your fault. And there is
the **Long Bell**, which is rung slowly and only twice a year on average: for a death,
or for a coach gone into the fen. Everyone in Larenne can tell the Long Bell from
the others while still asleep.

The Hollow Horn runs on those bells too, though it runs on them backward. The inn
wakes at the Trade Toll and does its real business between the Ash Bell and
midnight, which is why the doorsman's life is lived in the hours the rest of the
town has agreed to stop existing in. This is worth saying plainly: he sees Larenne
from its threshold and at its worst hour, and Larenne sees him the same way. The
town's opinion of him and his opinion of the town were both formed after dark, by
tired people.

### Market day

Loredas is market day. The moor farms come in along the coaching road with
peat, wool, hedgerow apples, and mutton that has walked to town on its own feet, and
the town buys, sells, gossips, and litigates in a single continuous motion around
the **market cross** — a squat granite pillar with the Eight cut into its faces, so
worn now that only Zenithar's scales and Kynareth's bird are still legible, which
Larenne considers appropriate to its priorities.

Bargains are witnessed rather than written. The **Market Court** convenes at the cross
between the Trade Toll and the Ash Bell, and it is not a court in any sense the
Kingdom of Daggerfall would recognise: it is an old man with an extraordinary memory
who recites the terms back at both parties until neither will admit to having meant
anything else. When a bargain is closed, both parties flick a copper into the
nearest banked hearth. These are **ash-coppers**; the Chapterhouse sifts them out on
Sundas and calls it a tithe, and everyone permits this fiction because the
alternative is a written tax.

Market day food is eaten standing up. **Renne-cake** is the town's staple and its
minor point of pride — a coarse oatcake baked on a griddle stone and folded, still
too hot, around mutton dripping and a smear of heather honey. There is **moor-eel**
soused in vinegar and sold from a barrel, which visitors find alarming and which
Larenne children eat like sweets. There is small beer, cut with well-water, drunk
from a shared horn cup on a string; and there is the strong stuff, which the Horn's
regulars call **Direnni-water** because, they say, it takes an elf's constitution to
survive it. The joke is very old. It is told to every new coachman as though it were
new, and the coachmen have learned to laugh.

### The inn's own law

The Hollow Horn has customs older than its current innkeeper, and they are the
customs of a *coaching* inn — that is, an institution whose business is strangers.

**Hearth-price.** A traveller who comes in out of the rain sits at the fire for free
for as long as his cloak steams, and begins paying the moment it stops. No one
measures this. Everyone knows exactly when it has happened. It is the single most
Breton thing about the place: a genuine kindness with a precise expiry, publicly
observed.

**The hanging beam.** Blades come off at the door and go up on the beam, each with a
chalk mark. The doorsman is expected to remember every mark without consulting it,
and to hand each weapon back into the right hand on the way out, in the dark, to a
man who has been drinking for six hours. This is regarded as the actual skill of the
job. The stillness and the size are considered decoration.

**Named rooms.** The Horn does not number its rooms; it names them — the Cold Room,
the Widow's, the Nook, the Coachman's, and the one over the stable that has never
been called anything but Upstairs. A guest given the Widow's has been told something
about his standing without a word being said, and if he is Breton enough to know it,
he will pay more next time to avoid it.

**The backward cup.** When a regular dies, the next round in the common room is
poured with the hand turned over, wrist upward, and nobody drinks until the pouring
is done. It takes perhaps four seconds. It is the whole of the funeral, as far as
the Horn is concerned; the Chapterhouse can have the rest.

### Barony and commons

The Barony of Larenne governs by courtesy, which is to say by a series of customs
that would be intolerable if anyone called them laws. There is the **hearth-tax**,
counted by chimneys rather than heads, so that a crowded house pays what an empty
one does. There are the **road days** — three days a year when every household owes a
body to the coaching road's ditches and drains, and the Barony provides beer and a
priest, and the town works, and grumbles, and rather enjoys itself. And there is the
**table-summons**: the Baroness's right to call any townsman to answer questions at
her own table, over her own food, as a guest. Nobody has ever been arrested by a
table-summons. Nobody has ever refused one either.

The class line in Larenne is not drawn by wealth — the tanner is richer than two
knights — but by *whether you are expected to explain yourself*. Commons explain.
Nobility ask. The Chapterhouse is the hinge: it feeds the poor at the **Loredas dole**,
keeps the parish rolls, teaches letters to whichever children can be spared, and
patiently reminds the Barony of the Eight's opinion on matters where the Eight can
be got to agree with the Chapterhouse.

Magic here is domestic and unremarkable. A hedge-wife charms a sty off an eyelid for
two coppers; the Chapterhouse's restoration work is competent and free on high days;
and Bretons shrug off small curses the way they shrug off weather — *it slid off him
like a hedge-curse* is a compliment about a man's temper, not his blood. Larenne is
quietly proud of its Direnni-thinned ancestry in the way people are proud of a
great-grandmother's silver: unearned, unspent, unquestionably theirs.

### Sayings

*Peace-and-taxes* — the standard grumbled greeting, and the town's entire political
philosophy. *A Daggerfall answer* — one that is technically true and no use at all.
*Kept dry* — lucky. *May your cloak stop steaming* — either a blessing or an eviction,
entirely dependent on tone, and both meanings are always faintly present. *Give me
eight* — give me a moment (from counting the Divines). *Well-mannered as Larenne* —
what other towns say about it, and not entirely kindly.

### How a Breton town treats an Argonian doorsman

Not badly. This is the important and uncomfortable thing.

He is never refused service, never turned from a shop, never spat at in the street.
Larenne is far too well-mannered for that, and besides, it rather *likes* having him
— the Horn's Argonian is a civic possession, mentioned to visitors in the same
breath as the market cross and the good bridge. Cruelty here is not a fist. It is a
thousand small administrative and conversational facts, each individually
defensible.

He is always seated near the door, when he is seated at all, and no one remembers
deciding this. He draws his wash-water second, after the kitchen girls, which is not
a rule and has never been said aloud and has never once failed to happen. The
Chapterhouse's parish roll lists the town's souls by household and then, in a
separate hand at the bottom of the page, *beast-folk, resident, one* — a clerical
category some clerk in Daggerfall invented two centuries ago, carried forward
faithfully by every scribe since, meaning nothing personal by it. Brother Aumery has
lately taken to re-inking that line in a way that makes it harder to read, and has
not explained himself to anyone, including himself.

People compliment him by measuring him. *You speak beautifully* — said to a man who
has spoken Breton since before he can remember. *You're very gentle, for one of
yours.* *I always feel safe when you're on the door* — which is true, and is also a
way of saying that a thing on the door is a thing that has a place. Drunks and small
children occasionally call him *it*, and the room corrects them, briskly, every time
— not for his comfort but for its own, because Larenne does not care to think of
itself as the kind of town where that happens. He has learned to be grateful for the
correction anyway. Gratitude is cheaper than the alternative.

There is real warmth in it too, and that is what makes it hard to be angry at. The
tanner's wife leaves warm renne-cake on the doorpost on the coldest nights and has
never in nine years used his name. The Market Court's rhymer greets him with an
elaborate, perfect courtesy he extends to no other servant in town, and it is
courtesy, and it is also a fence, and both facts are true at once. The children have
a skipping rhyme about fen-lizards — *snap-jaw, snap-jaw, count your teeth* — which
they sing near him with no malice whatsoever, which is worse than malice, and which
he has never mentioned.

He is known. He is not known *by name* so much as by function. In a town where
belonging is a performance of manners, he performs very well, and everyone agrees he
belongs, and no one would be able to say what he thinks about anything.

## II. THE SUBMERGED WORLD: WHAT AN ARGONIAN CARRIES OUT OF BLACK MARSH

### The Hist

Everything Larenne believes about Argonians is wrong in an interesting way, because
Larenne believes Argonians are a people, and Argonians are — but they are a people
in the way a hand is a hand: attached to something.

In Black Marsh there are the Hist: trees, if one insists on the word, though they
are trees the way a river is water. The villages of the Saxhleel are built at their
roots. Hatchlings are brought to the root-pools and drink the sap, and the sap
shapes them — body and temper and something under temper — and this is not
metaphor, and it is not magic in any sense a Breton chapel or a Direnni text would
recognise. It is simply how Argonians are made. A hatchling's first year is said to
belong to the Hist and only afterward to its parents. Argonians say this the way
Bretons say a child is born under a Divine: as a fact so ordinary it is boring.

The Hist remember. That is the second thing, and the harder one. What one Argonian
knows, the tree may hold; what the tree holds, another may be given, generations
later, without ever having been told. Argonians do not find this eerie. They find
the Breton arrangement eerie — all those separate skulls, each one losing everything
it held the moment it stopped.

### Hatch-kin

Kinship in the Marsh runs sideways, not down. Those hatched in the same clutch, in
the same season, at the same root, are **hatch-kin**, and that bond outranks parentage
in nearly every way that matters: hatch-kin share names, obligations, debts, and
sometimes a single reputation among them. Parents are real and loved and secondary.

This is why the Breton question *who were your people?* lands wrong on an Argonian
ear. It is not a rude question. It is a badly-built one, like asking a man which of
his legs he walks with. The Marsh question is *which pool, which season, which
tree* — and an Argonian who cannot answer it is not embarrassed so much as
structurally incomplete, in a way that has no Cyrodilic word.

Names follow the same logic. An Argonian's Tamrielic name is a translation, usually
of a deed or a role or a thing the Hist noticed, and it is expected to change over a
life as the person does. A name that never changes means a life that never turned.
Many Argonians beyond the Marsh carry a **dry-name** — the name outsiders gave them,
or the mistranslation that stuck at a border post — and keep the **wet-name** private,
or have lost it, or were never given one at all.

### The green murmur

Those who have it do not talk about it, and this is not mystery-mongering; it is
that the vocabulary does not exist in any language Larenne speaks. The nearest an
Argonian will usually come is to say the green murmur is like a room you have left
the door open on. You are not in it. You can feel the draught.

Close to the Marsh it is a constant, low, companionable pressure — a sense of being
attended to. Far from it, it thins toward nothing, and what remains is peculiar and
easy to dismiss: a taste at the back of the mouth in early spring. A dream of
standing water and enormous roots, recurring, oddly specific, in a person who has
never seen either. An unwillingness to burn certain woods, with no reason offered.
An occasional flat certainty about something that cannot possibly be known. Argonians
in the diaspora tend to file all of this under homesickness, because homesickness is
a thing Bretons will accept as an explanation, and the truth would require an hour
and would not be believed at the end of it.

It is worth saying that Hist-communion is serious and half-alien and entirely real.
It is not superstition dressed up. It simply operates on assumptions — about memory,
about individuality, about what a life is for — that no chapel of the Eight has any
apparatus for holding.

### Root-cut

There is a Jel word for an Argonian raised away from the Hist, and the usual
Cyrodilic rendering is **root-cut**. In Jel it is descriptive and carries pity but no
insult. In Cyrodilic it cannot help sounding like an insult, and so Argonians in the
west generally do not translate it at all.

They come west for the ordinary reasons: the Dunmer slave-takers who worked the
Marsh's northern edge for centuries and sold what they took; debts; ships; the
Empire's roads, which go everywhere and are therefore used by everyone. Whatever the
route, the experience at the far end is the same. The root-cut keep small private
observances that they would find difficult to justify if asked. A piece of wood
carried in a pocket, unremarkable, which no one else may touch. A window left open
in spring regardless of the weather. Counting — in a language half-remembered, under
the breath, to a clutch-number that means nothing to anyone else — when frightened.
None of this is doctrine. It is what is left when the tree is four hundred miles
away and the practice has decayed into habit.

The absence expresses itself most sharply at Breton funerals. Argonians do not bury.
The dead go back to the Hist; there is nothing to put in a hole and no reason to
stand around a hole while it is filled. So an Argonian at a Chapterhouse burial in
Larenne, on a wet Loredas, among people being decent to him, finds that he has
nothing whatever to do with his hands — and that this, more than the rhymes or the
second bucket or *beast-folk, resident, one*, is the moment the distance becomes
unbearable and impossible to explain to a single person present.

And there is one more thing about the marked and the root-cut both, which nobody in
Larenne has any framework for and which the Argonians themselves state without
elaboration: the Hist do not mark a hatchling idly, and the Hist are patient, and a
thing set aside is not a thing forgotten. It is simply a thing that has not been
asked for yet.
